Abstract—Academic documents such as certificates and transcripts are majorly targeted by advanced digital forgery, raising
serious concerns for institutional trust and automated verification
systems. Among these, digital inpainting attacks—where original
text from the documents is removed and convincingly replaced
with error prone content—are especially difficult to detect because they dont leave any visible traces, even in high-resolution
scans. Traditional forensic methods like Error Level Analysis
(ELA), which rely on compression irregularities, often fail when
applied to clean, high-quality scanned documents.
To address these challenges, this paper presents SRM-Net,
a dual-stream deep learning framework designed to detect
inpainting-based forgeries and errors at the pixel level. The model
combines a noise-focused stream using fixed Spatial Rich Model
(SRM) filters to record subtle high-frequency residual changes,
and a semantic RGB stream built on a pre-trained ResNet-18
network to learn the document’s structure and visual context.
Features from both streams are combined and decoded using an
encoder-decoder network to generate a binary mask that localizes
tampered or forged regions.
Due to the lack of publicly available academic forgery datasets,
the model is trained and evaluated on a synthetically generated
dataset that simulates realistic text-erasure and replacement
scenarios. Experimental evaluation shows that the proposed
method achieves an F1-score of 0.91, recall of 97.5%, and pixellevel accuracy of 99.8%, demonstrating that combining noise
residual analysis with semantic feature learning is highly effective
for detecting even visually seamless inpainting attacks in scanned
academic documents.
Index Terms—Image Forensics, Document Forgery Detection,
Spatial Rich Model (SRM), Dual-Stream Neural Network, Digital Inpainting, Residual Noise Analysis, Academic Credential
Verification, Deep Learning.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Documents like certificates, grade sheets, and mark sheets
are considered to be primary proof of academic credentials
and can easily be verified/used during the time of admissions,
recruitment, or evaluation. Over the years, most of these papers

have gone through the process of digitalization. Although digitalization is a great advantage in itself, there also exist threats
of manipulations in these documents due to advancements in
image manipulation software.
Digital inpainting is one of the toughest techniques for document manipulation. In this attack, original text information,
like the names of students or their grades, is carefully erased
and replaced with new information. The inpainting technique
is used to smoothen the area around the erased information
so that there are no gaps, and the document is very hard to
detect. Thus, the forged documents become so real that they
can dupe experts.
Conventional forensic analysis of documents is mostly centered on detecting pixel errors caused by JPEG compression.
Other tools like Error Level Analysis look at the difference
in JPEG compression levels to detect manipulated portions of
an image. While these tools work well when the image is
of poor quality due to high JPEG compression Ratios, they
fail when used on high-quality scanned documents. Here, the
compression levels are insignificantly small; hence the forged
portion will smoothly blend with the rest of the image.
With the accelerated pace of advancements in deep learning,
convolutional neural networks have been used for successful
applications in the field of image analysis, including object
detection and segmentation. The models have the ability to
train for spatial patterns using the data. However, traditional
models which use the information available in the RGB images
are biasing more towards the semantic information, which
sometimes might not be adequate for the detection of finer
details in the process of inpainting. In fact, the process of
inpainting has more effects on the noise patterns at the higher
frequency levels.

a dual-stream deep learning architecture for digital inpaint-

II. RELATED WORKS
Computer vision-based image forensics has also received
considerable attention in the last few years, especially in the
context of image tampering and document alteration. Some of
the initial studies performed in this domain were related to the
detection of visible tampering in image processing techniques
like copy-move, image splicing, and image resampling.
Among them, the approaches based on compression forensics have represented the most widely used methods for the
detection of forgery. ELA is a representative technique that
localizes tampered regions by considering the differences in
JPEG compression artifacts. This kind of techniques might
perform well for either low-quality images or web-compressed
content, but their reliability drops significantly when dealing
with high-quality scanned documents where compression artifacts may either be uniform or negligible. For this reason,
subtler manipulations like digital inpainting remain problematic for compression-dependent methods.
Noise-based analysis has also found applications in image forensics. It has been shown in past studies that the
noise present in authentic images is usually disrupted by
the manipulation tasks performed on the image. The idea of
Spatial Rich Model (SRM) features was first considered for
image steganalysis to quantify the noise residues in the image.
However, it has also found applications in forgery detection.
It is effective in analyzing the noise removal effect of surface
smoothing, which is caused by image inpainting. [1].
With the rise of deep learning, the use of convolutional
neural networks in forgery localization tasks has gradually
gained momentum. Deep learning models, specifically CNN,
have proved effective in discriminative feature learning from
raw images, allowing for the localization of forgeries at the
pixel level. Encoder-decoder models have particularly gained
popularity for their potential to maintain spatial resolution
when doing dense regression. Nonetheless, the majority of
the existing models utilizing deep learning techniques solely
focus on RGB cues, leaving their susceptibility to noise-level
forgeries unaddressed.

III. P ROPOSED M ETHODOLOGY
This section presents the proposed SRM-Net framework
developed for detecting digital inpainting attacks in academic
documents. Overall, the model should achieve an accurate
localization of forged regions at the pixel level by jointly
analyzing semantic visual information and noise-level inconsistencies introduced during manipulation.
A. Overall Architecture
The architecture of the proposed system is dual-stream
encoder–decoder based; see Fig. 1. Here an RGB document
image of size 256 × 256 is fed into the model for processing
through two parallel streams of feature extraction. The first
stream focuses on analyzing noise residual using SRM filters,
while the second stream aims at extracting semantic and structural features from the RGB image through a CNN backbone.
Features from both streams are fused and passed through a
decoder network that generates a binary segmentation mask
indicating forged and non-forged regions.
B. Noise Stream Based on SRM Filters
The noise stream is designed to capture subtle artifacts
introduced by digital inpainting operations. Inpainting typically involves surface smoothing and noise suppression to
remove visible discontinuities, which alters the natural noise
distribution of the document image. To capture these artifacts,
the input RGB image is first passed through a set of nontrainable Spatial Rich Model (SRM) filters.
SRM filters act as high-pass filters that emphasize noise
residuals while suppressing semantic content. Unlike trainable
convolutional filters, SRM filters are fixed and are specifically
designed to highlight inconsistencies in local pixel correlations. The output of the SRM layer is then processed through
a sequence of convolutional blocks consisting of convolution,
batch normalization, and activation functions to progressively
downsample the feature maps and extract higher-level noise
representations.

D. Feature Fusion Strategy
After the feature extraction step, the outputs from the noise
stream and the RGB stream undergo a process where they
are aligned and concatenated along the channel dimension.
This approach allows the network to scrutinize the residual
patterns for noise as well as the visual semantic information
simultaneously, hence making the network sensitive to the
inpainting-based forgeries that the single feature representation
may not capture.
The combined feature maps are then used as input to the
decoder neural network that does pixel-level classification.

C. RGB Stream for Semantic Feature Extraction
The RGB stream involves extracting semantic and structural
information from a document image. The RGB stream uses a
ResNet-18 model as its backbone. This ResNet-18 model is
pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset. The usage of a pre-trained
network helps the model in understanding features associated
with text structure, characters, and a document layout.
The input RGB map is processed through the convolutional
layers in the ResNet-18 architecture, and the intermediate
feature maps are obtained from the encoder. The features
obtained include contextual features, which are necessary

The neural network decoder is developed on an architecture
that employs upsampling during the reconstruction of the
spatial resolution in the input image. It consists of a sequence
of layers comprising upsampling followed by a convolutional
operation that smooths out the fusion features for the recovery
of fine-grained details. The last series applies the sigmoid
function when creating a binary probability map using pixel
values closest to one in forged areas and zero in authentic
areas.
It is to be noted that the resolution of the output segmentation mask is the same as that of the image, thus giving an
accurate localization of the inpainted area. Such property of
the output makes this proposed approach useful for real-world
document verification, for which the identification of the exact
position of forgery is important.
IV. DATASET AND E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP
A. Dataset Preparation
A synthetic dataset was created for this study because there
are no publicly accessible datasets that specifically address
inpainting-based forgery detection in academic documents.
About 3,000 document images that are intended to mimic
actual scanned academic certificates in terms of background
texture, font style, and noise characteristics make up the
dataset.

B. Training, Validation, and Test Split
A split ratio of 70-15-15 was adopted to divide the data
into the training, validation, and testing sets respectively. The
validation set was utilized to monitor performance and for
hyperparameter tuning, and the testing set was reserved to
determine generalization abilities, and so was not modified.
C. Training Setup
Using paired document images and matching ground-truth
masks, the suggested SRM-Net model was trained under
supervision. Pixel-level classification was optimized using
binary cross-entropy loss as the objective function. Standard
data augmentation methods, such as random cropping and
slight intensity variations, were used to increase robustness
and decrease overfitting after the Adam optimizer was used
for training.
D. Evaluation Metrics
To measure the performances of these models, the standard
evaluation metrics used for segmentation: pixel accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 values, are used. Pixel accuracy measures
how many pixels are correctly classified, while the values
of precision and recall measure the efficacy of the model to
locate the forged region appropriately.F1 measures the efficacy
of either precision or recall, which is more applicable when
there is class imbalance concerning the location of the forgery
region.
E. Implementation Details
All the experiments were done in Python using the PyTorch
deep learning framework. Standard image processing libraries
were used for image preprocessing and mask generation. The
model was trained and evaluated on a local development
environment with GPU acceleration; the performance on the
validation set was monitored to ensure stable convergence
during training. sectionResults and Discussion

H. Observations
When applied to high-quality scanned academic documents,
the experimental results demonstrate the limitations of conventional compression-based forensic techniques. The suggested
SRM-Net framework successfully captures minute artifacts
brought about by digital inpainting operations by combining
semantic feature extraction from RGB images with noise
residual analysis using SRM filters. The results show great
potential for real-world academic document verification, despite the fact that the current evaluation is based on a synthetic
dataset. In the future, the dataset will be expanded to include
actual scanned documents, and generalization across various
document layouts and scanning conditions will be improved.
V. C ONCLUSION AND F UTURE W ORK
SRM-Net, a dual-stream deep learning framework for identifying and locating digital inpainting forgeries in scholarly
documents, was introduced in this paper. In order to jointly
capture high-level structural features introduced during manipulation and subtle noise residual inconsistencies, the suggested method combines an RGB-based semantic stream with
an SRM-based noise stream. The framework overcomes the
drawbacks of conventional compression-dependent forensic
methods and permits precise pixel-level forgery localization
by combining these complementary representations. Strong
performance was shown in the experimental evaluation on a
synthetically generated dataset, with high recall and overall
pixel accuracy. The suggested method effectively isolates
manipulated regions in high-quality scanned documents where
traditional techniques like Error Level Analysis are unable to
provide accurate localization, according to qualitative analysis.
Although it must be noted that even this assessment is
executed on a precision-crafted synthetic dataset that emulates
actual scenarios of inpainting, practical academic documents
may contain different levels of variation, such as printed
artifacts, illumination conditions, etc., and layout structures.
