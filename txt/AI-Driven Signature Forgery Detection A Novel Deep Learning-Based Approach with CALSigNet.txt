AI-Driven Signature Forgery Detection: A Novel
Deep Learning-Based Approach with CALSigNet

Abstract—The increased usage of forged signatures is posing
a major threat to the legitimacy of legal and financial documents,
which requires effective automated detection systems. This
paper introduces CALSigNet, a deep learning model using CNN,
Attention, and LSTM to improve signature forgery detection
accuracy. The model employs CNN for spatial feature extraction,
Attention to analyse important signature areas, and LSTM to
identify sequential patterns, overcoming the limitations of
conventional approaches that tend to neglect both spatial and
temporal dynamics. With a large dataset of signature images, the
proposed model was tested through intensive training, 5-fold
cross-validation, and comparison with baseline CNN models.
CALSigNet attained a high-test accuracy of 99.60%, which
surpassed that of a Basic CNN (97.60%) and a ResNet-like CNN
(98.40%), with a cross-validation mean accuracy of 95.44%
(±0.0086). The results highlighted CALSigNet's potential as a
scalable, accurate tool for signature verification in forensic
science, improving the reliability of legal and financial document
authentication.

Keywords—AI, Deep Learning, Signature Detection, Image
Classification, CALSigNet, CNN, Attention, LSTM, Forged
Signature, Prediction.

I.

INTRODUCTION

Today, in the technological domain, Artificial Intelligence
(AI) has evolved as a milestone. It has profoundly shaped the
future across different domains to unprecedented levels of
automation, accuracy, and efficiency. AI has revolutionized
industries by processing Big Data, making smart decisions, and
detecting patterns. It can also be an innovation tool in forensic
science [1], [2]. AI has found its way in the domain of forensic
science to provide new solutions for crime scene analysis,
document verification, and facial recognition [3]. Investigators
now have the ability of rapid analysis, with accuracy, so that
the evidence proves better in court sessions. AI has brought an
advancement to forensic science, and it has lifted human
capability to new heights. It is countering the rising difficulty
of modern-day crimes, which can no longer be overcome by
traditional methods [4], [5] .
The detection of forged signatures presents one such core
challenge for forensic science since it is such a common
challenge with profound impacts on legal and financial
frameworks. Forgery of signatures threatens the credibility of
contracts, wills, and financial transactions, resulting in frauds,

disputes, and large economic loss [6]. According to the
Association of Certified Fraud Examiners (ACFE), in 2022 [7],
billions of dollars of loss is caused by document fraud, such as
forged signatures, globally every year. It is important to detect
forged signatures because methods used for detection manually
by experts can be time-consuming, subjective, and prone to
human error, especially in detecting high-quality forgeries [8].
Therefore, it is necessary to develop automated detection
systems capable of providing reliability, scalability and
objectivity especially in legal and financial applications of
critical importance as decision of document authenticity can
also end disputes.
Deep learning, a sub field of Artificial Intelligence, is an
effective solution for addressing the stated problem of forged
signatures [9]. AI models can use neural networks to learn
complex patterns in signature images and distinguish subtle
differences between a genuine and forged sample with high
accuracy. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are superior
at extracting spatial features from images, whereas
sophisticated methods such as attention mechanisms and Long
Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units extend the model's capacity
to pay attention to important areas and absorb sequential
dependencies, respectively [10]. These capabilities position AI
as the most suited for signature verification to produce
trustworthy systems that can manage large datasets, generalize
across diverse styles of signatures, and provide objective
decisions in real time [11].
To solve the forged signature problem, we propose a novel
deep learning model CALSigNet (CNN-Attention-LSTM
Signature Network). CALSigNet is a combination of a CNN
backbone with an attention mechanism and an LSTM unit that
accurately distinguishes between forged and genuine signatures
[12]. The research shows the model’s superior performance
with rigorous testing by kfold cross-validation, baseline CNN
model comparison, and the model’s real-world applicability in
forensic science. This is achieved by leveraging the power of
AI with the personalized approach of CALSigNet to enhance
the ability to validate documents in legal and financial settings,
thereby promoting the use of AI in forensic sciences [13].

a barrier in this process. The deployment of these systems
becomes more complex due to ethical problems that necessitate

strong protective measures to create fair and secure and

III.

METHODOLOGY

A. Dataset Collection and Preparation
We sourced our data from an open-source repository. The
dataset contains 2152 signature image files that are divided
between training and test sets [23]. We have 762 forged and
887 real signatures in the training set (a total of 1649 samples),
and in the test set, we have 248 forged and 252 real signatures
(a total of 500 samples) as depicted in Figure 1 and Figure 2.
To train our models, we ran a preprocessing pipeline in Python
that employed TensorFlow and Keras. Loaded images were
transformed into grayscale, resized to the size of 128x128
pixels, and normalized to range [0, 1] by dividing pixel values
by 255 [24], [25]. Preprocessing was carried out on files
ending with .png, .jpg, or .jpeg in a case-insensitive fashion,
handled errors, and generated training (X_train, y_train) and
test (X_test, y_test) datasets of shapes (1649, 128, 128, 1) and
(500, 128, 128, 1), respectively. For forged, labels were
designated as 0 and genuine signatures as 1, with training data
shuffled to avoid order bias [26].

B. CALSigNet Proposed Architecture
We developed a hybrid deep learning model, called
CALSigNet to detect real and fake signatures, implemented via
Keras in TensorFlow. The architecture starts with an input
layer which can accept grayscale images of size (128, 128, 1),
followed by a CNN backbone consisting of three convolution
layers (32, 64, and 128 filters, 3x3 kernels, ReLU activation,
same padding) and MaxPooling2D layers (2x2 pool size),
downsampling the outputs to (16, 16, 128) [27]. Then, a
MultiHeadAttention layer (4 heads, key dimension 128)
concentrates on key signature attributes, for example, stroke
patterns, whose output is flattened to a 128-size vector through
GlobalAveragePooling2D [28].
The attention output is then reshaped to (1, 128) and input
into an LSTM layer with 64 units, which strengthens feature
aggregation with the ability to catch possible sequential

dependencies despite the fixed image context [29]. The model
finishes with a Dense output layer with sigmoid activation,
producing a probability score (≥ 0.5 for real, < 0.5 for fake).
Compiled with the Adam optimizer, binary cross-entropy loss,
and accuracy as the metric, this architecture integrates CNN's
spatial feature extraction, Attention's region-specific attention,
and LSTM's sequence modelling to enhance forgery detection
performance [30], [31].
C. Model Training and Validation
CALSigNet was trained on preprocessed training set
(X_train, y_train) for 20 epochs with batch size 32 with
validation on test set (X_test, y_test) to monitor generalization.
Performance was measured using the evaluate method with test
accuracy and loss, while Matplotlib plotted training and
validation accuracy/loss curves to test convergence and
overfitting [32]. To further analyse robustness, 5-fold crossvalidation was applied to the combined dataset (X_full, y_full,
2149 samples) employing scikit-learn's KFold (n_splits=5,
shuffle=True, random_state=42) [33]. For each fold, the split
dataset contained roughly 1719 training and 430 validation
samples; a new model instance that was trained for 10 epochs
(batch size 32) on the training subset, and validation accuracy
were monitored. Average accuracy and standard deviation
across folds provided a reasonable performance estimate,
demonstrating consistency under data variations [34].
D. Comparison with Baseline CNN Models and Signature
Prediction
To measure the effectiveness of the new model, we
benchmarked it against two baseline CNNs with the same
training (X_train, y_train) and test (X_test, y_test) datasets
[35]. The Basic CNN had two convolutional layers (32 and 64
filters, 3x3 kernels, ReLU) with 2x2 max-pooling, followed by
a Flatten layer, a 128-unit Dense layer with ReLU, and a
sigmoid output, representing a straightforward classification
strategy. The ResNet-like CNN comprised a starting 32-filter
convolutional layer, a residual block (two 32-filter layers and a
skip connection) [36], max-pooling, a 64-unit Dense layer, and
a sigmoid output, using residual learning for better gradient
flow. Both were trained over 10 epochs (batch size 32), and
their test accuracies were compared against the new model's,
highlighting the superiority of its Attention and LSTM units in
signature verification [37].
For actual application, we have built a function to
preprocess a signature image (grayscale, 128x128, normalized)
from a file path, resizes it to (1, 128, 128, 1), and passes it to
the loaded CALSigNet model. It returns a probability score,
labelling the signature as "Genuine" (≥ 0.5) or "Forged" (< 0.5)
with confidence levels, plotted together with the image using
Matplotlib.
IV.

RESULTS

A. Data Preparation and Model Overview
The dataset was preprocessed into train (1649 samples: 762
forged, 887 genuine) and test (500 samples: 248 fake, 252 real)
sets, with dimensions of (1649, 128, 128, 1) and (500, 128,
128, 1) after grayscaling, resizing to 128x128, and
normalization. These dimensions facilitate compatibility with

deep learning models. CALSigNet model consisted of an input
layer, three convolutional layers, a multi-head attention
mechanism, an LSTM layer, and a dense output layer, with
405,953 trainable parameters [38]. This lean architecture, with
most parameters in the convolutional and attention layers, is
appropriate for signature verification, striking a balance
between complexity and efficiency.
B. Model Training and Evaluation
The CALSigNet model was trained over 20 epochs (batch
size of 32) using 1649 training samples validated on 500 test
samples with clear improvements in performance. Accuracy to
training improved from 53.45% (Epoch 1) to 95.82% (Epoch
20) with loss diminishing from 0.6911 to 0.1027, while
increasing validation accuracy went from 50.40% to 99.60%
by Epoch 19 (reaching loss at 0.0291) [39], slightly declining
further to 0.0328 by Epoch 20. In Figure 3, accuracy was
increased quickly, converging at Epoch 5 (training: 92.69%,
validation: 97.80%) where curves plateau, with validation
exceeding and flatlining above 98% post-Epoch 9; loss curves
exhibited parallel training improvement but small validation
fluctuation, with overfitting at Epoch 12 (validation accuracy
falls to 94.00%, loss peaks to 0.2540) before reaching stability
below 0.05. Test accuracy reached 99.60%, outperforming
signature classification.[40].

VI. CONCLUSION
This study proposed CALSigNet, a novel deep learning
network incorporating CNNs, an Attention unit, and an LSTM
cell with an outstanding 99.60% test accuracy for detecting
forged signatures, superior to a Basic CNN (97.60%) and a
ResNet-inspired CNN (98.40%) and having a solid 95.44%
(±0.0086) mean accuracy under five-fold cross-validation.
Signature predictions emphasized its forensic potential with
confidences up to 0.9962, but lesser ones such as 0.5398 in
some scenarios show room for improvement. Leverage of
CNNs for spatial information, Attention for region-based
attention, and LSTM for sequential patterns, CALSigNet's
lightweight 1.55 MB architecture offers a scalable, robust
solution for authenticating legal and financial documents, antifraud, and trust building. Future improvements may include
data augmentation, ensemble methods, bigger datasets,
temporal stroke data, or real-time systems, in addition to
testing against proficient, random, and simulated forgery
methods, testing performance on larger datasets over various
real-world use cases for scalability, and integrating
interpretability methods such as SHAP and LIME to increase
model transparency and user confidence, further solidifying it
in AI-based forensic document authentication.

V.

DISCUSSION

The high accuracy of CALSigNet (99.60%) implies its use
as a forensic tool, but compatibility with current signature
verification systems used in legal and financial institutions can
increase its impact in real-world applications. An API
integration of CALSigNet system into DocuSign platforms and
financial institution workflows would enhance forgery
detection alongside human review mechanisms. To fulfill
industry requirements the system requires both compatibility
tests along with confidence level refinement at 0.5398 levels or
higher for increased market acceptance.
The feasibility of deploying CALSigNet must be evaluated
through a complexity computational analysis. The 1.55 MB file
size combined with training duration between 30 to 34 seconds
per epoch shows efficient operation yet evaluation of standard
hardware memory usage and inference time (between 0.431 to
0.991 seconds) will clarify system scalability. To make the
Attention mechanism practical for forensic applications
forensic operators must optimize its resource usage.
Web-based deployment of CALSigNet requires essential
cybersecurity evaluations to prevent vulnerabilities which
protect the system from threats. Security testing of data
encryption together with access control systems and adversarial
attack defenses would protect sensitive signature information
