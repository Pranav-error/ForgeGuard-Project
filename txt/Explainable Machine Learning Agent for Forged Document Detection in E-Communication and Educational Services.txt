Abstract— Since even small forgeries can have major
ethical and managerial ramifications, the quick development of
electronic communication and web-based learning services
makes document verification more difficult. Time-consuming
manual checking is ineffective against complex forgeries. To
close this gap, this paper proposes an Explainable Machine
Learning Agent that leverages a refined ResNet50 model to
identify forged documents and signatures. The proposed
model uses a deep residual learning-based spatial feature
extractor with SHAP-based interpretability that allows high
accuracy along with transparency in decisions. The model was
trained on an 80-20 stratified split of the combined dataset of
handwritten signatures, namely CEDAR, BHSig260–Bengali,
and BHSig260–Hindi. Key performance indicators for
accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUROC were used to
test the results. With an overall accuracy of 95% and an AUC
of 0.96, this design surpassed previous benchmarks such
VGG19, MobileNetV2, Random Forest, and Logistic
Regression. In addition to performance, SHAP explainability
analysis offered feature attributions that could be interpreted
for forecast tracking and auditing. Therefore, the agent
provides a workable, intelligible method that can be readily
implemented for document forgery detection in real-world ecommunications and educational verification systems.

II.

METHODOLOGY

A. Dataset description
In this study, we used the Handwritten Signature Datasets
fetched from Kaggle, which consolidate three widely used
offline benchmarks for signature verification: CEDAR (Latin
script; 55 writers, 24 genuine and 24 forged signatures per
writer), BHSig260-Bengali (Bengali script; 100 writers, 24
genuine and 30 forged per writer), and BHSig260-Hindi
(Hindi script; 160 writers, 24 genuine and 30 forged per
writer). All samples are offline scanned images organized in
a consistent folder hierarchy by subject ID (e.g.,
DATASET/1/images, DATASET/2/images, …), facilitating
deterministic splits and per-writer evaluation. For
reproducibility, we specifically reference the BHSig260Bengali directory provided in the Kaggle bundle, which
contains 100 subject folders, and we pair it with CEDAR and
BHSig260-Hindi to enable cross-script experiments spanning
Latin, Bengali, and Hindi signatures.

Deep learning-based methods for digital document

while they have achieved varied degrees of accuracy, they
frequently lack interpretability.

Rouis (2024) [8] introduced a Deep Discriminative
Feature Learning model that improved representation
learning yet failed to exceed 95% accuracy on noisy datasets.
With an accuracy of 93.46%, a CNN-based study that
contrasted pre-trained models like ResNet-101 and VGG16
demonstrated the power of CNNs but also their limited
resilience [9]. For multimedia post-editing detection, Jonker
et al. (2024) [10] employed transfer learning with VGG16;
this method had a 75% accuracy rate, but there were
problems with text-level document forgeries. Similarly, the
Noisesniffer model, which focusses on noise inconsistency
for forgery detection, provided interpretability but performed
poorly when compressed [11]. CNNs, transformers, and
hybrid frameworks are only a few of the conventional and
contemporary techniques that were assembled in a TechRxiv
survey on fraudulent document detection conducted between
2024 and 2025. A lack of a consistent, interpretable strategy
that is appropriate for a variety of real-world datasets is
shown by the survey's findings that the majority of models
obtained accuracies between 70% and 94% [12]. Barglazan
et al. (2024) [13] highlighted instability across datasets with
accuracies ranging from 70% to 94%.

Random Forest (RF), MobileNetV2, and VGG19—against

Agent. All models operate on identically preprocessed,

Evaluation follows standard verification metrics (Accuracy,

A. Experimental Setup
All experiment was performed on a workstation with
Intel® Core™ i7 (8 cores, 3.2 GHz), 32 GB RAM, and a
single NVIDIA GPU (RTX 3060, 12 GB). For
reproducibility, outputs were checked for consistency on
Google Colab Pro with Tesla T4 (16 GB). The stack
consisted of Python 3.10, TensorFlow/Keras 2.15 (finetuning ResNet50, VGG19, MobileNetV2), scikit-learn 1.4
(metrics, train/test split), XGBoost 2.0 (embedding-level
classifier for SHAP), SHAP 0.45 (Tree/GradientExplainer),
NumPy/Pandas (data manipulation), Matplotlib (plots), and
OpenCV (I/O and pre-processing). OS: Ubuntu 22.04 LTS
with CUDA 12.x and cuDNN compatible with TensorFlow's
build. The Kaggle handwritten-signature dataset was divided
using a stratified 80/20 split (subject-balanced) to avoid class
skew. Images were resized to network input (ResNet50:
224×224), channel triplicated to grayscale-to-RGB, and
normalized to [0,1]. Adam (lr = 1e-4) with batch size 32 was
used to train the models for up to 40 epochs with
ReduceLROnPlateau and EarlyStopping (patience = 6). GAP
embeddings from fine-tuned ResNet50 were extracted for
SHAP, and the downstream XGBoost decision function was
explained with an interventional background of 200 train
embeddings. All the comparisons were performed on the
20% held-out test set. Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score,
and AUROC were the primary metrics, which were reported
for each of the models; confusion matrices, ROC curves, and
SHAP visualizations complemented the quantitative
evaluation.

robustness and dependability of the suggested framework.

C. Training & Validation Performance Analysis
Training and validation accuracy and loss curves of the
proposed ResNet50-based Explainable ML Agent as shown
in Fig. 3. The curves altogether indicate that there is a
smooth and generalizable learning process during
optimization.

This distribution supports the metrics presented in
Section V-A by confirming that the model shows a balanced
trade-off between precision and recall. The residual learning
structure's ability to capture the unique stroke irregularities,
pressure discontinuities, and curvature distortions present in
forged handwriting is demonstrated by the high classification
consistency between the two classes.

With residual layers capturing the three most salient
features of authenticity, namely stroke orientation, penpressure uniformity, and contour consistency, accuracy
increases rapidly in the first few epochs. The training and
validation accuracies stabilize above 0.96 after the tenth
epoch, with a slight divergence between them, thus
indicating that the deep spatial representations learned are
invariant to variations in handwriting. Loss curves support
this hypothesis by showing a steep drop in loss during the
beginning and gradual saturation toward 0.06 for the training
loss. The validation loss also follows the same trend;
however, there are minor fluctuations in this curve due to
intra-class variability within handwriting. The small gap
between the two curves shows the effectiveness of batch
normalization, learning rate scheduling, and early stopping
regularization in avoiding numerical overflow and gradient
explosion.

V.

CONCLUSION

In order to identify signature forgeries in academic
documents and electronic communications, this work
presented an Explainable Machine Learning Agent built on
the ResNet50 architecture.
The suggested model
outperformed baseline methods such as VGG19,
MobileNetV2, Random Forest, and Logistic Regression,
achieving an exceptional accuracy of 95%. The system is
reliable for real-world verification tasks because of its mix of
SHAP and Grad-CAM visualisations, which provide
transparency and interpretability.
The model's resilience and dependability were
demonstrated by the confusion matrix and ROC evaluations,
which verified consistent generalisation and low
misclassification.
Larger and more varied datasets, transformer-based
vision models for deeper contextual learning, and multimodal fusion integrating textual and visual clues for
sophisticated counterfeit detection are some ways that future
research might expand the framework.
