Abstract— In the global digital era, we encounter ourselves
in the necessary of ascertaining the secure issuance and
verification of certificates, as these are crucial to checking
credibility among institutions and preventing fraud. Traditional
Paper-based and centralized digital systems are at High risk of
forgery, duplication, and data breaches, emphasizing the
requirement for a strong, scalable, tamper-proof framework.
Therefore, in this paper, an advanced two-way glide path for
certificate management is proposed that desegregates
blockchain technology, cryptography, fuzzy logic, and artificial
intelligence (AI) to accomplish more secure and round-the-clock
issuance, as well as intelligent verification. In this paper, the
appendage of issuance creates unique cryptographic hashes ,
Universally Unique Identifiers (UUIDs) , and digital signatures,
then cuts QR codes and uses fuzzy signatures to endure small
repugnance‟s from physical or digital documents. Certificates
are stored on a decentralized infrastructure (IPFS) and dropped
anchor to blockchain networks for verifiable records. The
verification process applied a two-stage methodology that
combined blockchain validation with AI analysis, changing the
use of Optical Character Recognition (OCR) and deep learning
toolsets to verify both metadata integrity and visual
authenticity. A fuzzy logic decision layer synthesizes these
analyses, relegating certificates as Valid, Valid with Low
Confidence, or Invalid. Experimental evaluation evidences the
system’s superior performance, achieving a fraud detection
accuracy of 97.2%, an average verification latency of 3.8
seconds, perfect recall (zero false negatives), and high
discriminative power with ROC AUC of 0.9804 and PR-AUC of
0.9770. The proposed framework provides a scalable, secure,
and user-friendly solution applicable across education,
corporate, healthcare, government, and recruitment sectors.
Future enhancements may include smart contract integration,
multi-chain interoperability, and resilience against adversarial
attacks, positioning the system as a future-ready platform for
global certificate management and verification.

III. PROPOSED METHDOLDOGY
This research employs a dual-path methodology for
secure digital certificate management, consisting of two
tightly integrated yet distinct processes: Issuance and
Verification. Both processes are reinforced through the
convergence of blockchain technology, cryptographic
algorithms, fuzzy logic, and artificial intelligence (AI),
ensuring that certificates are not only securely generated but
also verifiable in a transparent, tamper-resistant, and
intelligent manner. The overall methodology is depicted in
Figure 1, which illustrates the step-by-step flow from
certificate creation to its final verification, emphasizing the
interplay between security mechanisms and AI-powered
validation.
Step 1: Collection of Certificate Metadata. The Certificate
Issuance process begins with the collection of certificate
metadata, which includes attributes such as the recipient’s
name, issuing authority, course or program information,
issued and expiration dates, and a serial or institutional
reference code. After this data is ready, the system produces
two fundamental security elements: a cryptographic hash and
a UUID. The cryptographic hash (such as SHA-256) is a
digital fingerprint of the certificate data and therefore
provides integrity in case any change has occurred, because
each small alteration would produce an entirely different
hash. The UUID helps maintain this by providing an
internationally unique tag that prevents references from being
duplicated across institutions or contexts.
Additionally, a digital signature is applied using the
issuer's private key to secure further the certificate, which
contains a digital trust anchor that can be verified against the
public key of the prospect. As an added benefit, a QR code is
incorporated into the design of the certificate, containing a
compressed summary and a link to the blockchain record for
easy verification. In addition to these standard cryptographic
steps, our technique includes a novel fuzzy signature layer.
Unlike a firm digital signature, the fuzzy signature resists
some slight changes or destruction in the physical and digital
certificates. For instance, even in the case where the
certificate is scanned, poorly photographed, or OCR (Optical
Character Recognition) misreads occur, and the fuzzy
matching system – using OCR and metadata checks – can be
employed where others would not allow such a certificate to
be recognized and verified. This is the key feature that makes
the framework robust to real-life conditions when documents
won't always stay immaculate.
After embedding these multilayered protections
then the data from there certificate is uploaded on a stroke
infrastructure which is decentralized, as like the
InterPlanetary File System (IPFS), with Pinata ensuring
reliable pinning and retrieval of data. So, to obtain
immutability and public transparency, the certificate record is
then devoted to a blockchain network, with the
Sepolia/Ethereum Testnet serving as the favored
environment for data-based deployment. Once this grounding
is complete, the issuance process concludes, acquiring a
certificate that is cryptographically secured, redundantly
stored, and immutably recorded .
The Certificate Verification process begins when a
user, employer, or institution attempts to validate a

certificate. Verification may be initiated by scanning the
embedded QR code or by extracting textual content from a
physical or digital certificate through OCR. The extracted
metadata is then cross-checked against the blockchain record
in two stages: first, by comparing the certificate metadata
with the blockchain-stored metadata; and second, by
validating the complete certificate against its on-chain
representation. This ensures both surface-level integrity and
deep structural consistency.

Figure 1. Result Methodology

Following blockchain validation, the procedure
proceeds to the AI verification pipeline. First, a general
intelligent model generates a commensurate analysis to
evaluate the certificate in its entirety, assessing blatant
anomalies [60]. This is then refined by more applicationtailored deep learning models—such as CNNs,
MobileNetV2, or Transformer-based architectures—that
examine the certificate in a fine-grained manner [61]. They
can pick up on fine-grained distinctions, including changes to
fonts or logos, shifts of watermarks that don’t quite fit, or
discrepancies in electronic seals. After the AI models make
their evaluations, those are piped into a fuzzy logic decisionsupport layer that combines results from both blockchain and
AI pipelines.In contrast to a binary decision process , fuzzy
logic suggests the possibility of allowing the system to sort
certificates into somewhat graduated categories: Valid, Valid
with Low Confidence (indicating an opportunity may exist
but no critical inconsistency), and Invalid (which could
require manual review). As shown in Figure 1, the two-way
method enables a healthy business environment for
establishing a digital certificate service. The generation
process is secure, as the certificates are generated in a unique
form and recorded in an unchangeable manner. Guidance by
AI-enabled analysis, fuzzy decision-making support, and
blockchain verification can provide multiple layers of
protection against technology forgery. Together, these paths
create a scalable, trusted, and intelligent solution that

IV. CONLCUSION
Our Proposed system delivers a Fraud Detection Accuracy of
97.2% with an Average Verification Latency of just 3.8
seconds, making it well-suited for real-time applications. The
model ensures perfect recall (no false negatives) and
demonstrates strong discriminative ability with ROC AUC of
0.9804 and PR-AUC of 0.9770. These results highlight its
reliability, efficiency, and potential for secure digital
certificate verification. Future work should focus on
scalability, testing with larger and more diverse datasets, and
exploring deployment on distributed frameworks like
blockchain to maintain speed under heavy loads. Real-world
applications could include e-commerce payment security or
access control in critical infrastructure. Although only 10
false positives were observed, refining the model to reduce
them without compromising recall remains an important
direction. Additionally, strengthening resilience against
adversarial attacks will be essential for long-term robustness.
