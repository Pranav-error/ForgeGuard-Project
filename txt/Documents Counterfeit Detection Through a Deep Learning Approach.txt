Abstract—The main topic of this work is on the detection of
counterfeit documents and especially banknotes. We propose an
end-to-end learning model using a deep learning approach based
on Adapnet++ which manages feature extraction at multiple scale
levels using several residual units. Unlike previous models based
on regions of interest (ROI) and high-resolution documents, our
network is feed with simple input images (i.e., a single patch) and
we do not need high resolution images. Besides, discriminative
regions can be visualized at different scales. Our network learns
by itself which regions of interest predict the better results.
Experimental results show that we are competitive compared with
the state-of-the-art and our deep neural network has good ability
to generalize and can be applied to other kind of documents like
identity or administrative one.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Forensic science is the application of scientific knowledge
and methodology to legal problems and criminal investigations1 . Forensic research in document examination is a
hot topic still open. Due to the rapid development of new
information technology and the wide use of the Internet,
digital information is obtained easily. Digital content can be
illegally manipulated for some malicious issues such as forged
documents or banknotes counterfeit. Nowadays, with the digital technology available to each one it becomes available
even to untrained users to make a fake document. Therefore,
counterfeiting is an increasing problem for both government
agencies and private companies. From an economical point of
view it can cause substantial havoc.
In the field of image forensics, major research efforts have
been made for security applications focused on image forgery
detection and authentication [1]. Some works have inspired
researches in documents forensic where efficient applications
have been proposed for documents and banknotes counterfeiting detection. Particularly, most of the approaches are based
on visual features and texture characteristics. Most of the
cases, documents are forged applying cut and paste techniques
to manipulate figures, letters, or words. In [2], authors use
uniform Local Binary Patterns (LBP) to capture copy-paste
or deletion operations through discontinuities in the texture.
However, the approach needs some preprocessing to define
patch (ROI) around each textual connected component and to

geometric features to identify false documents [3], [4].
Identifying the source printer of a document is another
important purpose in forgery detection. The ability to discriminate between original documents and their photocopies
is an important task since original digital content can be
illegally manipulated and freely printed into documents. In this
perspective, printer feature characteristics have been proposed.
For instance, DCT coefficients [5] have been used as a
fingerprint for the printing technology used to create the document. In [6], 15 statistical features are proposed to describe
pattern noise produced by 20 flatbed scanners. The approach
is subject to a fine segmentation between the foreground
and the background (text segmentation and noise filtering).
A deep learning approach based on Convolutional Neural
Networks (CNNs) is proposed in [7] to learn automatically the
best features which characterize the printer source. Reported
results between a feature-based SVM and the proposed CNN
classifiers show comparable classification performances for
scanned documents but on microscopic input images, SVM is
clearly better. Furthermore, an interesting comparative study
on texture descriptors [8] to differentiate between genuine
and counterfeit documents on several ROI textures show that
performances of several texture hand-crafted descriptors are
comparable to CNN descriptors.
Other works come back to the physical phenomena and
focused on spectroscopic techniques for banknotes counterfeiting. Non-destructive analysis techniques are deployed to acquire information about the chemical composition of genuine
and forged materials. Spectral analysis (for instance infrared or
X-ray spectroscopy) allows to discriminate between genuine
and counterfeit banknotes [9], [10]. Baek et al. [11] focused on
a special sensor for ATM applications where RGB and infrared
images are produced. A classifier is feeded with some local
features defined on particular circular and rectangular regions.
Sensors that are capable to identify forged banknotes are
rather expensive. Some methods are defined for non-controlled
environment where a mobile device is used for documents
acquisition [12]–[15]. Dimitti et al. [14] proposed the HOG
descriptor computed inside image patches surrounding SURF
interest points. HOG features are classified using a SVM identifying genuine vs fake banknotes. Banknotes were captured

This paper is in the same vein on deep learning in a noncontrolled environment, but our network is different. Mainly,
we use a full image as input to our network. In [15] two
images (genuine vs counterfeit) are alternatively compared
on randomly cropped regions. Furthermore, our model uses
residual units for better multi-scale feature extraction allowing
good abilities to manage low resolution images. Our main
contributions include the following aspects.
We propose an end-to-end trainable deep model the
counterfeit banknote detection task, which differs from
the literature by using full banknote image and operations
for rich multi-scale feature extraction.
• We present a quantitative analysis (what happens inside
our network) of the discriminative regions on banknotes,
which on some scales, are similar to the preselected
patches used in the state-of-the-art.
• We identify new discriminatory regions for counterfeit
banknote detection which could be injected into existing
datasets.

Section IV is devoted to the conclusion and we give some
perspectives to our work.

B. Class Unbalanced
A challenge in the banknote counterfeit detection task is
the unbalanced dataset that we have in the training stage [8].
Due to the nature of the data, these always present a lack of
counterfeit samples. By having a fewer number of counterfeit
samples, the network can fall into overfitting and degenerate
the model. To address the unbalanced problem, techniques
most used are on the dataset, i.e., resampling (over-sampling
and under-sampling), and techniques on loss functions, i.e.,
class weighted. Because resampling tends to lead to overfitting,
we chose to use the class weighted technique. This technique
gives higher weight to minority class and lower weight to
the majority class. Among these, we selected the median
frequency balancing [19] since it gave us the best empirical
results. The median frequency balancing is defined by:

C. Network Structure
In recent years, the use of Convolutional Neural Networks
(CNNs) has led to several improvements in many fields of
knowledge with outstanding results, especially in computational vision. Thus, ResNet [17], [20] architecture achieves
good performances on several benchmarks [21], [22]. Fewer
parameters and better performance make ResNet become
popular in the community. However, we not only need a model
with few parameters and faster forward-pass time’s, which are
critical attributes for deploying models. We also need a model
capable of doing a high-resolution feature extraction on the
multi-scale level. On the other hand, AdapNet++ [16] proposes
a novel technique to efficiently generate multi-scale features
throughout the network without increasing the number of parameters. For this reason, we took the encoder of AdapNet++
based on ResNet-50, which meets the previous requirements,
and we modified it for the banknote counterfeit detection task.
In the model presented in Fig. 2, we opte to preserve the
resolution while reducing the depth of the layers. This is
mainly in block 4 and in the Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling
(eASPP) [16] operations, managing to make the model ≈ 10%
slightest. Furthermore, at the end of the network, we add four
convolutions of size 3 × 3, 7 × 7, 3 × 3, and 3 × 3 to merge

For better classifier performance, the last four convolutions

Note that to capture high-resolution features, we use a larger
receptive field (i.e., larger kernel). In other words, we need
a wider viewing area in the image. For example, in highresolution images, using small receptive fields (e.g., orange
kernel in Fig. 3) does not provide enough information to
classify banknotes. Thus, to achieve the feature extraction in
high-resolution, we apply Atrous Convolution [23] (i.e., sparse
convolutions), which can have the same parameter number
(i.e., 3 × 3), but its main characteristics are the dispersion rate
parameter s which provides the convolution the capacity to
extract features from a larger viewing area (see Fig. 3).
Performing the sparse convolutions is not enough for robust
feature extraction in the banknotes. Although, when using
sparse convolutions, we obtain a larger area of vision compared to the original convolutions, they still present a single
scale vision (e.g., s = 2). For this reason, we apply Atrous
Spatial Pyramid Pooling (eASPP), which performs sparse
convolution operations in parallel with a different rate of
dispersion (s = {0, 3, 6, 12}) without sacrificing efficiency
and capturing features in higher resolution (i.e., multi-scale
vision).
This way, we keep the higher resolution details while aggregating the same amount of context as before. Furthermore,
we want to obtain a rich feature extraction at different levels
of scale. This is because, in banknotes, we can obtain different
types of information at several scales (see Section III-D). This
function is developed by multi-scale units (residual units A,
B, C, D, E from Fig. 2). These units (B, C, D, E), unlike the
typical residual unit A used in ResNet, extract, and combine
multi-resolution information.
The residual units in Fig. 2 are used in different resolutions
(i.e., sample size by downsampling), allowing them to process
information at multiple scales, and also to conduct operations
with sparse convolutions.

III. E XPERIMENTS
Very few datasets [15], [24] are available in the litterature.
This lack of public ressources is mainly due government data
protection rules. For this work, we carry out our experiments
on a camera-based Euro banknote dataset [18].
Our experiments were conducted for entire images, unlike
traditional methods that use patches (i.e., ROIs). Our model
achieves promising results considering that we create a model
that classifies all banknotes at the same time, i.e., a single
model capable of banknote counterfeit detection independent
of the type (B1, B2, C1, C2, D1, D2) or side (O, R) of the
banknote. We also localize the regions used by the model to
analyze the results. Next, we first introduce the dataset and
the hyperparameters we use in this work. Then, we perform a
series of experiments and, we analyze discriminative regions
provided by our network on different scales.

B. Experimental Setup
We implement our algorithm using the well-known deep
learning framework of Tensorflow. The proposed network is
built on AdapNet++. The Atrous convolution is used to have
a receptive field of large sizes. The different residual units are
used to obtain varied information through various scales. Due
to the different number of samples for each type of banknotes,
our number of iterations also changes. Models are trained for
616 iterations for B1, B2, with 440 iterations for C1, C2,
and 540 iterations for D1, D2. In addition to the combined
types of B3, C3, D3, BCD3, we use 1424, 1424, 1592, 5570
iterations, respectively. The model is training using Adam
optimizer, with a learning rate of 1e − 4 and batch size 2
in all the reported experiments. Each input image is cropped
and up-scaled to a size of 500 × 1000 pixels. Experiments
have been conducted on NVIDIA GTX Titan X with 12 GB
of memory.

To better analyze the generalizability of our classifier, we
plotted the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves
(Fig. 4), for databases that combine the different types of
banknotes (i.e., B3, C3, D3, BCD3). We can see the behavior
(classification) of our network for the various folds of the
dataset in the graphics. Here we can highlight that our model
presents a low variance due to the good use of the parameters
(i.e., our network appropriately uses the trainable variables
to achieve a robust feature extraction). Besides, our network
possesses a high increasing rate for TP (our model predicts
positive class correctly). Fig. 4 shows that the C3 database
(from C1 + C2) is especially challenging. Note, the FP
(our model incorrectly predicts positive class) appears very
early in the ROC curve. We attribute this behavior to noise
and external components (e.g., shadow) that reduce feature
extraction efficiency. In addition, in Table III, we demonstrate
the stable behavior of our model by presenting a relatively
high response to the different classification metrics. A more
detailed analysis (recall, F1-score from Table III) shows that
particularly for class C3, our model tends to classify false
banknotes as originals. This behavior can be corrected by
better capturing the input image. The statement is supported
by the results of the other data types (B3, D3) by having a
direct relationship between outcomes and sample purity (i.e.,
images without external components, rotation, motion, noise,
shadow).

We present an end-to-end trainable model based on Adapnet++, which manages feature extraction at multiple scale
levels using several residual units. We solve the problem
of unbalance inherent of banknotes datasets by using the
median frequency balance on the loss function, thus providing
a higher penalty on counterfeit samples. Unlike benchmarks
that work with textures on predefined regions of banknotes
(i.e., patches), we present an independent model of these,
and internally we show that our network uses similar regions
or close at different scales, to perform counterfeit banknote
detection. Besides, new regions used to make the detection
are visualized. We believe that it would be beneficial to add
these as patches for the previous models. Our network has
been efficiently designed achieving an acceptable prediction
time also, due to the reduction of parameters required by our
model. Results with the state-of-the-art are competitive, taking
into account that we use a simple input image (i.e., a single
patch) and without the high resolution required by previous
models. Furthermore, our deep neural network shows good
abilities when reporting results combining all the different
types of datasets.
Finally, we believe that this work can be applied to other
kind of documents (for instance identity and/or administrative
one) and will guide future research in the search for real-time
models with a higher generalization capacity.
