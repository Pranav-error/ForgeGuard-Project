Abstract - Copy-move forgeries often exploit homogeneous
regions in images with large-scale attacks to either highlight or
conceal target objects. These manipulations are simple to
execute but challenging to notice. Forgery detection techniques
like Copy-Move Forgery Detection (CMFD) cannot detect these
forged documents because they are unable to identify a
sufficient number of effective keypoints in homogeneous areas,
leading to inaccurate and inefficient results. SURF stands for
Speeded-Up Robust Features and is used in this paper along
with A-KAZE and Scale-Invariant Feature Transforms.
According to our experiment, A-KAZE offers superior detection
performance under diverse attacks, especially when it comes to
large-scale attacks targeting homogeneous regions. A-KAZE is
found to be more accurate than SIFT, SURF, and A-KAZE when
applied to the NB-CASIA dataset, achieving detection
accuracies of 89.2%, 93.9% and 98.98%.)

It is convenient for novices and experts to use simplistic
image-editing Software, which allows them to make authentic
alterations. Among these tweaks, some are made for
entertainment, while others are done maliciously [3], [4].
Digital images are uncertain because of this fact. These digital
images become even more problematic when they are used for
specific purposes such as news broadcasts, research journals,
and courtrooms.
Forgeries involving copy-move are also known as copypaste forgeries or cloning forgeries. They involve copying and
pasting segments within an image [5], [6]. It is common for
forgers to conceal or emphasize an element in an image. In
order to create convincingly forged documents, imageprocessing operations like JPEG compression, noise addition,
rotation, and scaling may be used during copying and moving.
Due to the ease with which copy-move operations can now be
conducted, Software for image editing has become
increasingly important and active with CMFD [7], [8]. The
two most commonly used methods for detecting copy-moves
are block-based detection and interest keypoint detection [9],
[10].

CONCLUSION

Changing the location of an image on the screen conceals
tampering without being obvious. These types of attacks can
tamper with images, which makes forensics concerned about
their authenticity. Image manipulation has become easier over
the past few years thanks to powerful software development.
The use of forgery detection techniques has been the subject
of many research papers. It is also impossible to detect smooth
forged regions with keypoint-based methods when there are
insufficient keypoints. The forgery of copy-moves is
discussed in this work. We evaluated three keypoint-based
approaches: SIFT SURF and A-KAZE. Our experiments
demonstrated that the A-KAZE scheme significantly
outperforms the others, providing superior detection
performance under various attack scenarios. The A-KAZE
approach excels particularly in detecting forgeries within
homogeneous regions affected by large-scale attacks. When
applied to the NB-CASIA dataset, A-KAZE achieved the
highest detection accuracy at 98.98%, compared to 89.2%
with SIFT and 93.9% with SURF, while also maintaining the
lowest computational cost. A-KAZE has the potential to offer
substantial improvements over traditional methods when it
comes to copy-move forgery detection, as demonstrated by
these results. This advancement can enhance the reliability
and effectiveness of CMFD tools in various practical
applications, ensuring better integrity and authenticity of
digital images.
