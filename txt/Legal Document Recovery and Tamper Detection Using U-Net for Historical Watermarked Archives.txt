Abstract— Legal documents preserved in historical archives
are often damaged due to aging, environmental factors, and
physical degradation, especially those with embedded
watermarks. Recovering these documents while ensuring their
authenticity is essential for legal, historical, and institutional
purposes. Existing methods for document recovery and tamper
detection struggle with low accuracy in reconstructing severely
degraded texts and often fail to detect subtle forgeries or
inconsistencies in watermarks. To overcome these limitations,
this study proposes a U-Net-Based Dual-Channel Image
Restoration and Tamper Detection (DCIR-TD) framework. The
first channel restores document fidelity through image
inpainting and denoising. The second channel simultaneously
detects tampered regions by analyzing spatial inconsistencies
and watermark distortions using deep feature map
comparisons. The proposed method is applied to colonial-era
legal documents, effectively recovering faded content and
exposing unauthorized modifications. Experimental results
demonstrate that DCIR-TD achieves high restoration accuracy
by 96.5% and, tamper localization performance by 97.8%, peak
signal-to-noise ratio by 98.6%, structural similarity index
measure by 96.8%, outperforming traditional image processing
and machine learning techniques. This framework provides a
robust and automated approach to preserving the integrity of
historical legal archives and authenticating their contents for
legal verification and digital archiving.
Keywords: U-Net, legal document restoration, tamper detection,
historical archives, watermark verification, deep learning.

I.

INTRODUCTION

Legal documentation in historical archives provides
important documents recording land ownership, legislative
actions, contracts and administrative actions from prior
centuries. Legal documents are often marked with
watermarks, which are used to authenticate other legal
documents and to provide reference points in land tensions,
legal inheritances of property issues, and historical research
[1]. However, because of the fragility and length of exposure

to environmental elements, much of the legal documents
suffer from deteriorating inks, torn condition, water
condition, and/or bleed-through of inks. The introduction of
items created and maintained in the digital realm has added
another concern: the possibility of tampering during the
archival process, which could lead to a loss of perception and
the legitimacy of all records[2].
In terms of restoring documents, traditional restoration
processes (manual reconstruction and image processing
methods) are tedious and generally insufficient for troubled
sources that cannot be converted entirely or physically
recovered[3]. The typical tamper detection solutions that
exist utilize rule-based models that not only fail to identify
sophisticated manipulations but may also fail to identify
when the integrity of a watermark is being subtly
modified[4]. Each of these shreds of evidence is a clear
indication of a need for a solution that provides intelligent
and automated content recovery and tamper detection, as well
as the recovery of visual integrity for any historical
documents[5].
This paper outlines a novel Design of U-Net-Based DCIRTD to combat these issues. U-Net is an outstanding
architecture of convolutional neural networks, particularly
adapted from its origin for biomedical image segmentation,
which has been extensively studied, explored, and applied in
numerous image-to-image tasks that require increased
precision[6]. The DCIR-TD, as a dual-channel scheme,
utilizes U-Net for its dual-process capability. The first
channel performs restoration through document image
inpainting and document image denoising to help recover the
missing and degraded parts of the document[7]. The second
channel utilizes the marked image, such as watermark
patterns or text structures, by analyzing the watermarking
feature maps to determine tampered areas. Therefore, the
DCIR-TD approach is also guaranteed to recover the original
content while identifying and detecting fraudulent deeds[8].

III.

METHODOLOGY

A. U-Net-Based Dual-Channel Image Restoration and
Tamper Detection

BACKGROUND

Restoration of legal documents and tamper detection are
essential to maintain the integrity of historical archives.
Degradation, fading, and alteration of the legal document are
detrimental, making traditional restoration methods difficult.
Using a U-Net-based deep learning approach enables the
restoration of degraded content and the identification of
tampered areas, thereby ensuring the integrity and reliability
of legal documents that contain historical watermarks.
A. Dual-Channel Image Restoration (DCIR)
This study improves upon U-Net for the task of restoring
historical documents by including attention mechanisms to
better focus on degraded or noisy text regions. This study is
essential for demonstrating how deep learning can recover
fine-grained details of text dialogue or text chunks in decadeold and damaged legal or archival documents, as shown by
Wang Y. et al.[10].

semantic segmentation using a U-Net variant. It demonstrates
how image segmentation can detect inconsistencies, such as
unauthorized editing in legal documents, and aligns with the
goals of tamper detection. Vision Transformers (ViTs) were
utilized to model long-range dependencies within the textures

IV.

EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

A. Dataset Description
The collections used for recovering legal documents and
detecting tampering include Tobacco800 for historical legal
archives, PRImA and DIBCO for the restoration of degraded
documents, and DEFACTO for analysis of tampered
documents[19]. The RVL-CDIP collection offers a diverse
range of legal document categories, and the IAM-HistDB
collection also includes handwritten archives[20]. Synthetic
collections can also be created by embedding digital
watermarks and inserting tampering into clean legal
documents for use as ground truth, thereby enabling pixellevel annotations for building U-Net-based detection and
restoration models.
B. Simulation Environment
The simulation environment operates using Python with
TensorFlow or PyTorch performing the U-Net
implementation[21]. Document images are preprocessed in
OpenCV by adding noise, simulating watermarks, and
injecting tampering[22]. In a supervised setting, ground-truth
masks were utilized in the segmentation process. The
performance measures used were PSNR, SSIM, and pixel-bypixel accuracy[23]. The training, validation, and visualization

V.

DISCUSSION

Due to age and exposure to environmental stressors,
historical legal documents are prone to damage, resulting in
the loss of important information and the authenticity of the
document[4]. Addressing issues of reliable recovery and
tamper detection is essential for preservation and remediation
in a court of law[25]. This study presents a dual-channel, UNet-based approach for recovering degraded content and
detecting tampering[26]. Such a combined process, while still
under development, will be valuable for enhancing archival
integrity and forensic certainty[27].

Fig. 4 illustrates the tamper localization performance,
assessing the framework's ability to effectively detect and
mark modified or counterfeit parts of legal documents[31].
The DCIR-TD model achieved a 97.8% level of accuracy by
examining spatial inconsistencies and distortions in
watermarks through deep feature map comparisons[32]. This
level of precision provides a degree of confidence when
detecting minor copy manipulations, ultimately better
preserving the authenticity and forensic reliability of legal
archives with historical value[33].

CONCLUSION

The U-Net-based DCIR-TD framework addresses a timecritical and important need to preserve, restore, and
authenticate historic legal records, often incorporating the
watermark. The framework achieved this through image
inpainting and denoising, while tampered detection accuracy
for detecting and localizing tampered areas relied on deep
feature-based tampering detection. Overall, the final
restoration of the image using this methodology was quite
impressive, with some severely degraded content being
successfully restored. The
DCIR-TD
framework
outperformed conventional digital image-processing
techniques and traditional machine learning methods in the
application of colonial archive records. While the DCIR-TD
framework is fully automated, and incredibly effective in
aiding with the preservation of legal documents while
enabling strong forensic verification permissible by the
watermark on the original document, tools of this nature
carries valuable implications for legal institutions and for
archived digitization programs; in particular with primary
focus on the authenticity and memorialization of priceless
historic legal documents. Ultimately, the proposed DCIR-TD
framework holds tremendous potential for preserving and
restoring the authenticity and durability of historically
significant legal records.
