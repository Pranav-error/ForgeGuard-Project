Automated signature inspection and forgery
detection utilizing VGG-16: a deep convolutional
neural network

Abstract— With the growing usage of handwritten signatures in
legal documents and financial transactions, it is crucial to select an
efficient technique for validating these signatures and eliminating
forgeries that might cause considerable losses for clients. Offline
signature authentication is yet challenging despite extensive study,
notably when trying to separate expert forgeries from signature pools.
This research aims to develop an automated system using VGG-16 a
deep convolution neural network (DNN) to successfully tackle
handwritten signature fraud. The automated system is developed
separately by utilizing a pre-trained and a trainable VGG-16, whose
performance is accessed over a widely used signature dataset
comprising an equal number of authentic and fake signatures. In
comparison to the pre-trained VGG-16 model, the trainable VGG-16
based model can yield a testing accuracy of 0.962, which is about
17.75% higher. The simulation outcomes decisively illustrate that the
recommended methodology is suitable for automatically spotting
fraud handwritten signature images.
Keywords— CNN, VGG-16, Classification, forgery identification

I. INTRODUCTION
The use of signatures is a common way for individuals to
formally bind a document or express their preference for
specific information, and is frequently used in financial and
legal activities such as bank letters, property documents,
checks, and corporate documents [1]. However, signature
forgery has become a major cybercrime, with individuals
attempting to duplicate signatures for financial gain. In recent
years, the rapid growth of technology has made signature
forgery more sophisticated, making it difficult to detect with
traditional methods. As a result, advanced techniques such as
deep learning algorithms are necessary to accurately identify
forged signatures.
A variety of forgery detection systems built on deep
learning has been presented in the literature. In [1], a
straightforward CNN including three convolution and
maxpooling layers setup alternatively with one flattened and
dense layer is suggested for spotting forgeries of signatures.
The author created a dataset with 6000 signatures with three
persons. In order to be fed into the CNN, originally collected

RGB format signatures are first transformed to grey scale and
then
to
bitmap
format.
For model evaluation, accuracy and loss percentage metrics ar
e utilized. Two DNNs, one developed using transfer learning
and another customized one are compared with traditional
SVM and KNN techniques for signature recognition in [2].
Clearly, the DNN models beat the conventional ones when
results from two DNNs were compared over a dataset of 600
signatures generated from 30 persons. In [3], the author
suggests a CNN model for forgery detection that combines the
Harris-Surf and Crest-Trough algorithms. The suggested CNN
model architecture is composed of 5 layers, and the model is
trained using the back-propagation technique. The CEDAR
dataset, which has 1320 photos for each authentic and fake
signature, is used to test the model. Siamese neural network a
variety of DNN is suggested by the authors of [4] as a possible
solution to signature verification problem. Transforming to
grey scale, image scaling, morphological erosion and
augmentation are used as part of pre-processing stage. The
model performance is then compared with LeNet and SigNet
on an artificially created signature dataset. In [5], another
DNN is proposed for signature verification that uses the
ResNet-50 with some more layers and encoders. A Kaggle
dataset having 2660 English signatures is used in the model’s
testing. The use of Mutual Signature DenseNet (MSDN) in a
region-based DNN is investigated in [6]. It is made up of two
identically structured CNN branches with same learnable
parameters. The MSDN feature extractor is used in their
suggested technique to analyze numerous local regions, and
the similarity between two signatures is then used to train a
Siamese Neural Network (SNN). The model is then evaluated
by comparing it to several CNN architectures, including VGG16, AlexNet, and ResNet-18. A tailored four layered CNN
optimized using ADAM optimizer has shown a validation
accuracy of 88.39% over a synthetic dataset with 770
signatures prepared from 55 persons each one contributing 7
genuine and 7 forged one in [7]. The authors of [8] have
experimented with a CNN model considering various split
ratios of datasets to identify fake signatures. Accuracy and

After creating a handwritten signature dataset using attendance

The goal of this project is to develop a simple VGG-16
based automated signature verification system (ASVS) that
can identify whether a given signature is genuine or
fraudulent. With given a signature image as input, the model
will use the various convolution layers of VGG-16 to extract
essential features and then utilizing these features will classify
the signature at the output layer. Taking the publicly available
CEDAR dataset as a reference, the performance of ASVS
modeled with a trainable VGG-16 and with a pre-trained
VGG-16 is accessed with respect to four assessment metrics,
such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The
simulation outcomes convincingly indicate the viability of the
proposed technology in recognizing forgery in the handwritten
signature visuals.
The working mechanism of the ASVS is explained in the
next part. Section 3 analyzes the outcomes followed by
conclusion in next paragraph.
II. ASVS USING VGG-16
Fig. 1 depicts the overall working principle of the VGG-16
based ASVS. To assess the effectiveness of the proposed, a
standard database of signatures is required. For this purpose,
publicly available handwritten signatures related to 55 persons
are collected from https://cedar.buffalo.edu/NIJ/data. To
ensure that that the performance of the system should not be
biased by the size and slant offered in the signature, the
collected signature images need pre-processing. So in the first
step of data pre-processing, to attain homogeneity across the
images, which ranged from 200px to 800px in size, we
adopted OpenCV’s resize function setting the interpolation
parameter as INTER_LINEAR and rescaled the images to a
standard resolution of 250px to 250px prescribed for the
VGG-16 model. After that, noise removal from images is
accomplished utilizing a Gaussian blur filter. The
fastNlMeansDenoising function of the OpenCV is used to
apply the filter. Following the pre-processing, a predefined
splitting ration was used to divide the dataset into training and
testing sets.

Then a VGG-16 based DNN developed using transfer
learning is utilized for classification of the signature images as
genuine or forged. Numerous studies have been inspired by
VGG-16 to utilize it to address a range of object detection and
classification problems [2][7][12]. It has already shown 92.7%
accuracy to classify 1000 images of 1000 different categories.
The 13 convolution layers, 5 maxpooling layers and 3 dense
layers form the 21 layers of VGG-16, but 16 of them are
weight layers with learnable parameters. The network has total
14,764,866 parameters out of which 50,178 are trainable
parameters and remaining 14,714,688 are non-trainable ones.
Convolution produces a fixed number of feature maps from
the input image, which are then fed into a max pooling layer to
produce pooled feature maps. The subsequent convolution
layer receives these pooled feature maps, and so on until we
attain at the final max pooling layer. The pooled feature map
is finally flattened and fed into the fully connected layers. In
the first part of this study, the effectiveness of a pre-trained
VGG-16 network that was previously trained over the
‘imagenet’ dataset with thousands of classes of images is
accessed for forgery detection in signatures. The fully
connected layer i.e. the top layer of pre-trained VGG-16
model normally generates 1000 different output labels, but the
chosen problem in this study is a binary classification work.
So instead of entirely utilizing the architecture of pre-trained
VGG-16 network, we have customized the network with a
new top portion i.e. the fully connected layer and output layer
by freezing the pre-trained convolution layers and unfreezing
the top fully connected layer of original VGG-16 network.
The pre-trained convolution layers of VGG-16 will help to
convolve visual features from the input preprocessed signature
images. Then the non-frozen pre-trained layers are trained
with the collected signature images. In the next part of the
study, instead of utilizing the pre-trained weights of the
convolution layers, the entire VGG-16 model with new top
portion is trained with the signature dataset and its
classification performance is accessed.
III. EXPERIMAENTAL RESULT DISCUSSION
A computer system with an AMD Ryzen 5 3600 CPU clocked
at 4.2Ghz, Nvidia RTX 2060 6GB GPU(Graphics Processing
Unit) and 16GB of 3200mhz RAM was used to implement the
suggested ASVS. The study made use of Python 3.9.5 with the
OpenCV image processing library, Tensorflow version 2.10,
Matplotlib and SK.learn library.
The handwritten signatures were retrieved from the freely
accessible CEDAR dataset. There are 2640 grey scale
signatures altogether in this dataset, 1320 of which are real
and 1320 of which are forgeries. We have also added 60
images of each real and fake signature images of our own,
making the total signatures for the dataset to be 2760. Then all
the images are rescaled to a size of 250X250. The original and
preprocessed image is shown in Fig. 2. Then the dataset is
divided in a proportion of 80:20 and the preprocessed images
from the training set are given as input to the VGG-16 model.

Finally the prediction outcome of the model for two
random images one belong to each class is depicted in Fig. 8
that was obtained by using the Model.predict () function from
the tensorflow library. the Model.preditct() returns a tuple of
probabilistic predicted value in terms of (X,Y), where X
indicates the probability of the image belonging to class 0,
which is forgery in this case, and Y indicates the probability of
the image belonging to class 1 which is labeled as genuine for
this case. Comparison between the two probabilistic values
was made. If X was found to be greater than Y, it implied that
the image belonged to class 0, the forgery class, and if Y was
found to be greater than X, it implied that the image belong
to class 1, the genuine class. The Model prediction returns the
probabilistic tuple [X,Y] with corresponding values
[1.0679432e-06, 9.9999893e-01] for the case of a random
image as shown in Fig.8 (a). Value of Y was found to be
greater than X which meant the signature image is a genuine
signature. Additionally, the random image shown in Fig. 8(a)
is searched in the CEDAR dataset manually is found in the
‘full_org’ directory named as ‘genuine_29_12’ which further
confirmed that the prediction made by the model is indeed
correct. Similarly the model prediction returns the

IV. CONCLUSION
In this research, verification of signatures and identifying
the forged one among them is addressed as a binary
classification task. The key point is the comparison of the

attainment of the pre-trained and trainable version of VGG-16
model for the same task. By conducting experiments on the
well-known CEDAR dataset and examining the outcomes, it is
clear that the trainable VGg-16 based ASVS is better able to
identify forged images from the image pool than the pretrained model. To further advance this research, data
augmentation can be adopted to yield a massive synthetic
dataset for improving the performance of the model. Another
research direction that we plan to perform is to design a less
complex CNN model with fewer parameters to achieve higher
accuracy in detecting forgery in both online and offline
handwritten signature verification process.
