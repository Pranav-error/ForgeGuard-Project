ABSTRACT Image tampering detection and localization have become critical tasks in digital forensics due
to the widespread accessibility of powerful image editing tools. We came up with a new Vision Transformer
(ViT)-based design that uses patch embedding and multi-head self-attention mechanisms to efficiently find
fake documents. This is because we need lightweight but highly accurate detection systems that can model
all of a document’s features. Our model uses a modular pipeline made up of a patch embedding layer,
four transformer encoder blocks, and a streamlined classification head. The slimmed-down classification
head consisted of only 69,793 trainable parameters to keep the computation costs as minimal as possible.
Experimental evaluation conducted on four benchmark datasets—CASIA v1, DEFACTO (Splicing), MICCF2000, and Columbia—demonstrates the superior performance of the proposed model. The model gets an
AUC of 0.9712 and an F1 score of 0.9587 on CASIA v1, an almost perfect AUC of 0.9993 and an F1 score
of 0.9914 on DEFACTO, a good AUC of 0.8491 and an F1 score of 0.8319 on Columbia, and an exeptional
AUC of 0.9804 and an F1 score of 0.9097 on MICC-F2000. The proposed architecture was evaluated against
59 different models and demonstrated its effectiveness, showing its strong potential for practical applications
such as detecting and localizing document forgeries.
INDEX TERMS Image tampering detection, lightweight deep learning model, vision transformer, selfattention mechanism, patch embedding.

Image tampering detection and localization has become an
essential field in digital forensics, focused on finding and
recognizing tampered areas inside images [1]. The quick
development of image editing tools and AI-generated forgeries have made proof of content authenticity progressively
harder. Original methods depended on handcrafted features
and statistical analysis, but modern techniques include
deep learning and transformer-based architecture [2]. These

applied ConvNeXt with multi-scale integration, obtaining
superior results but facing issues with tampering regions
and requiring significant computation. A lightweight Form
model [13] utilized Vision Mamba for global modeling,
reducing FLOPs but performing poorly in semantic segmentation and deepfake evaluation.
These methods integrated attention mechanisms and
frequency-domain insights. The CANF model [14] combined
five noise properties with channel and frequency attention,
offering strong and modular performance but being resourceintensive. A self-adversarial training pipeline [15] included
a forgery attention module, improving durability and limited detection, though it relied on stationary, handcrafted
filters. TANet [15] merged CNNs and Transformers with
edge-refinement blocks (BAB, RAMB), achieving better
boundary precision at the cost of complexity. ReLoc [16]
merged restoration and localization modules for enhanced
post-processing performance, though it suffered from a high
inference period.

a simple perceptual hashing architecture, proving efficient
and generalizable but poor in localization and challenged by
subtle manipulations.

numerical (metric tables) comparisons confirm the
model’s superior accuracy, generalization, and scalability in forgery detection and localization.

III. PROPOSED MODEL ARCHITECTURE

The entire architecture of the Vision Transformer as shown
in Fig. 2 begins with an input RGB image of size 224 ×
224 × 3, a standard dimension for image classification
tasks. This image is first passed through a Patch Embedding
Block, which divides the image into smaller, non-overlapping
patches and transforms each patch into a vector.In particular,
the image is split into 16 × 16 patches, giving a total of
196 patches (because 224/16 = 14 and 142 = 196). Each
patch is embedded into a 32-dimensional space, creating
a matrix of shape 196 × 32, where each row is a patch
embedding.
These patch embeddings are then sequentially passed
through four Transformer Blocks. Each block captures
contextual relationships among patches using self-attention
mechanisms. Unlike CNNs that extract features locally, transformers allow global information exchange from the very
first layer. After these transformer layers, the output remains
196 × 32, which is then processed by a Global Average
Pooling layer. This operation reduces the representation to a
single 32-dimensional vector by averaging across all tokens.
The condensed vector is then input into a fully connected
classification head consisting of dense layers. This head maps
the learned representation to the final output class.In general,
the model is based on a modular pipeline of patch embedding,
transformer-based feature extraction, and classification.
The input image I ∈ RH ×W ×C , where H = W = 224 and
C = 3, is partitioned into a grid of fixed-size non-overlapping
patches of dimension P × P, with P = 16.

In the Vision Transformer architecture (Fig. 4), the Transformer Encoder swaps convolutional layers with a attentionbased framework. The model contains L = 4 stacked encoder
layers, every single layer containing two vital modules:
Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) and a Position-wise
Feedforward Network (FFN). Each module is bound by
residual connections and Layer Normalization, creating a
residual learning block. This design permits the network to
learn about long-range dependencies and global interactions
among non-local parts of the input image without depending
on the inductive biases fundamental to convolution.
Each Transformer Block comprises two main subcomponents: a Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) mechanism
and a Feed-Forward Network (FFN), both enclosed within
residual connections and preceded by Layer Normalization.
The input to each transformer block is a matrix of shape
196 × 32.
The first operation is Layer Normalization, normalization
across features to improve efficiency and stability. Normalized embeddings are then passed on to the MHSA module,
where attention scores over all tokens are calculated such
that the model can attend different parts of the image. The
attention output is then added to the original input via a
residual connection.
Following this, the output undergoes a second Layer
Normalization and is passed through a two-layer FFN. The
first dense layer expands the feature size from 32 to 64,

the model’s equal effectiveness in recognizing splicingbased manipulations. The high AUC implies better differentiation among authentic and tampered samples. CASIA
v1 includes both grayscale and color images subjected
to lossy JPEG compression, complicating the detection of
spliced regions. The ViT’s global self-attention mechanism
solves this issue by capturing non-local dependencies and
spatial inconsistencies that CNNs might miss due to their
restricted receptive fields. Moreover, the positional encodings
retain spatial connections across patches, making the model
attuned to boundary-level aberrations due to splicing. This
enables the ViT to accurately identify tampering, even when
abnormalities are subtle or spread across distant regions in
the image. Furthermore, the confusion matrix for the CASIA

challenging conditions, demonstrating both high sensitivity
and specificity toward forgery detection.
B. ANALYSIS ON DEFACTO SPLICING DATASET

The DEFACTO Splicing dataset showed outstanding results
across all benchmarks, with the ViT attaining 99% accuracy
as shown in Fig. 5b, 99% precision, 99% recall, and
F1 score of 0.99 as shown in Fig. 6b, and an AUC of
0.99 as shown in Fig. 8b. The nearly perfect outcomes
illustrate the model’s resilience in analyzing uncompressed,
high-resolution images with modest splicing manipulations.
This dataset’s accurate preservation of low-level textures
and lack of compression noise allowed the ViT to use
extensive token-wise feature representations. The multi-head
focus system allows the collection of various representations
of spatial relationships, assisting the model in accurately
identifying manipulated areas. In addition, training for
80 epochs certainly helped the model to refine attention
weights and positional encodings, thereby optimizing both
detection sensitivity and specificity. The results obtained
demonstrate the ViT’s ability to be used as a high-resolution
forgery detector in optimal image conditions. Moreover,
the confusion matrix for the DEFACTO Splicing dataset,
as illustrated in Fig. 7b, displays almost perfect diagonal
dominance, with minimal false positives and false negatives.
The extremely low rate of misclassification highlights
the model’s precision in distinguishing authentic images
from tampered ones. This strong performance confirms
the model’s capability to accurately capture and localize
fine-grained manipulations even in high-quality, minimally
altered images.
C. ANALYSIS ON COLUMBIA DATASET

The Columbia dataset presented a more significant issue
owing to its uncompressed TIFF format and reduced dataset
size. The ViT model, after 150 epochs of training, achieved an
accuracy of 82.65% as shown in Fig. 5c, precision of 80.34%,
157682

recall of 86.24%, and F1 score of 0.8319 as shown in Fig. 6c,
and an AUC of 0.8491 as shown in Fig. 8c. Despite being
significantly lower than in other datasets, these values remain
competitive and significantly outperform the performance of
many previous methods. The model’s comparatively higher
recall implies robust sensitivity for detecting manipulated
images, though the marginally reduced precision indicates
occasional misclassification of authentic samples. This is
because of a lack of diversity of augmentations in the dataset
and the decreased rate of explicit manipulation artifacts. Still,
the transformer’s capability to build long-range dependencies
among uniform image backgrounds permits it to outperform traditional CNN-based models. The extended training
duration certainly reduced the dataset’s size limitations by
permitting more refined tuning of inter-patch interactions and
token embedding weights. In addition, the confusion matrix
for the Columbia dataset, shown in Fig. 7c, reflects a modest
increase in misclassifications compared to other datasets,
with some authentic images incorrectly labeled as tampered
and vice versa. However, the majority of the samples were
still correctly classified, and the diagonal dominance was
maintained, confirming the ViT model’s general capability
to handle challenging, less diverse datasets with minimal
overfitting.

D. ANALYSIS ON MICC-F2000 DATASET

The MICC-F2000 dataset contains high-resolution images
exhibiting several forgery techniques. The ViT model, trained
for 200 epochs, obtained an accuracy of 96.06% as shown in
Fig. 5d, precision of 88.51%, recall of 93.57%, and F1 score
of 0.9097 as shown in Fig. 6d, and an AUC of 0.9804 as
shown in Fig. 8d. These results are particularly significant due
to the dataset’s inherent complexity, which includes different
manipulation strategies and subtle semantic inconsistencies.
The high recall implies that the model successfully detects
a variety of tampering types, including both overt and

deeper localization models such as TransForensics and
TDA-Net, having F1 values below 0.63 (Table. 1). The
performance is attributed to ViT’s architecture, it captures
non-local dependencies with self-attention and encodes
spatial continuity through learnable positional embeddings,
which resulted in major resilience to texture variance and
compression flaws found in CASIA v1.
