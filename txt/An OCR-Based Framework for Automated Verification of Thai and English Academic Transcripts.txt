Abstract—Academic transcripts are essential documents for
employment and higher education but remain susceptible to
forgery and manipulation, creating a need for efficient and
reliable verification methods. Traditional verification is often
time-consuming due to the structural complexity of transcripts
and the challenges of extracting information from PDF and
image formats. This study presents an OCR-based framework
for automated transcript verification that integrates preprocessing and postprocessing techniques to enhance data extraction
quality. English transcripts were evaluated using OCR models
PaddleOCR, Tesseract, and EasyOCR, while Thai transcripts
were assessed with Tesseract and EasyOCR. Experimental
results demonstrate that preprocessing substantially improves
extraction accuracy and that postprocessing further refines the
outputs. Among the evaluated models, PaddleOCR achieved
the highest performance on English transcripts with an overall
accuracy of 81.87%, whereas Tesseract yielded the best accuracy
for Thai transcripts at 81.11%. These findings underscore the
effectiveness of combining OCR with tailored preprocessing
and postprocessing strategies to support reliable and efficient
transcript verification in academic settings.
Index Terms—Optical character recognition (OCR), PaddleOCR, Tesseract, EasyOCR, Academic transcripts, Document
classification.

I. I NTRODUCTION
In the current landscape of employment and higher education, applicants are typically required to submit supporting
documents such as resumes, identification, certificates, and
academic transcripts. Among these, transcripts play a critical
role in validating academic achievements and qualifications.
However, they are also vulnerable to alteration and forgery,
creating a pressing need for reliable and efficient verification
mechanisms.
Transcript verification at King Mongkut’s Institute of Technology Ladkrabang (KMITL) is still conducted through conventional channels. External organizations—such as companies or universities—submit inquiries via email or postal
mail to the registrar’s office, where staff manually compare
the submitted documents, usually in PDF or scanned form,
against the official database. Although transcripts at KMITL
follow a standardized layout, this approach remains slow,

error-prone, and increasingly burdensome, with verification
requests already exceeding 2,500 cases annually and continuing to rise.
Optical Character Recognition (OCR) technology provides
a promising pathway to address these challenges by automatically extracting textual information from non-editable transcript files (e.g., PDFs or scanned images) and converting it
into structured digital data. When combined with appropriate
preprocessing to enhance image quality and postprocessing to
refine textual outputs, OCR enables accurate alignment with
institutional records, thereby accelerating verification and
reducing the likelihood of human error. The adoption of OCRbased verification can thus transform transcript validation at
KMITL into a faster, more accurate, and scalable system
that strengthens institutional efficiency and builds trust with
external stakeholders.
This study develops and evaluates an OCR-based framework specifically tailored for KMITL’s transcript verification.
The framework integrates preprocessing techniques, applies
multiple OCR models for both Thai and English transcripts,
and incorporates postprocessing methods to improve accuracy.
Experimental evaluations were conducted on widely used
OCR models, including PaddleOCR [1], Tesseract [2], and
EasyOCR [3]. Results show that preprocessing and postprocessing substantially enhance extraction quality, with PaddleOCR achieving the highest accuracy for English transcripts
and Tesseract performing best for Thai transcripts. Overall,
this research not only delivers a practical solution for transcript verification at KMITL but also establishes a foundation
for scalable applications in academic institutions, contributing toward secure, efficient, and technology-driven academic
document verification in higher education institutions.
II. R ELATED W ORKS
Optical Character Recognition (OCR) has long served as
a foundational technology for digitizing textual content. By
converting printed or handwritten text in images or scanned
documents into machine-readable formats, OCR enables efficient storage, retrieval, and analysis of paper- and imagebased records. Modern OCR engines, enhanced by machine

learning and deep learning, have demonstrated robust recognition capabilities across diverse fonts, layouts, and even
handwritten styles. Beyond digitization, OCR has increasingly
been integrated into higher-level applications such as information extraction, natural language processing (NLP), and
document understanding.
Research has also focused on addressing language-specific
challenges within OCR-based pipelines. For example, a notable study investigated Thai spelling correction from OCR
word outputs [4]. The Thai language presents unique challenges due to its complex script system—44 consonants, 12
vowels, 4 tone marks, and 10 special characters—together
with the absence of explicit word boundaries. These characteristics complicate downstream applications such as sentiment
analysis, machine translation, and OCR postprocessing. The
study introduced a two-stage framework of error detection and
correction. Using 133,757 sentences from the Thai Digital
Collection (TDC), containing over 224,000 erroneous syllables, synthetic text-image datasets were generated across
multiple fonts and sizes to simulate OCR scenarios. Tesseract
OCR was used to extract text, exposing common errors such
as missing characters and improper line breaks. Experimental
comparisons showed that Bi-LSTM achieved the best performance for error detection. These results underscore the role
of advanced deep learning methods in enhancing Thai OCR
postprocessing.
Beyond language-specific tasks, general-purpose OCRbased approaches have targeted structured information extraction. The FUNSD dataset [5], derived from RVL-CDIP,
provides annotated forms with entities labeled as key, value,
header, or other, along with semantic relationships. While the
dataset proved useful, annotation inconsistencies limited its
broader applicability. Subsequent work revised the dataset and
introduced U-Net models enhanced with Channel-Invariant
Deformable Convolutions for improved key-value pair detection. These efforts highlight how spatial layout analysis and
structured annotations can augment OCR-based approaches
for practical document understanding.
In contrast to OCR-dependent pipelines, recent studies have
advanced OCR-free methods for document understanding.
The Document Understanding Transformer (DONUT) [6]
was introduced as an end-to-end framework that directly
encodes document images into structured outputs such as
JSON, thereby eliminating the need for OCR. By pretraining
on large-scale datasets, including IIT-CDIP and the synthetic
SynthDoG corpus, DONUT demonstrated superior accuracy,
efficiency, and generalizability compared to OCR-based visual
document understanding systems, marking a pivotal step
toward scalable OCR-free solutions. Building on this line of
work, an OCR-free approach for identity document information extraction [7] combined a Swin Transformer encoder with
a BART decoder to jointly capture visual layouts and semantic
content. To mitigate data scarcity, the study generated more
than 7,000 synthetic documents in addition to 969 real Italian
identity records. Experimental results showed that models pretrained with synthetic data substantially outperformed those

trained only on real data, particularly in low-resource scenarios. While OCR-free methods have proven highly effective in
structured domains such as identity documents, preliminary
experiments in applying DONUT to educational transcripts
suggest challenges in generalizing to the more complex and
heterogeneous layouts of transcript data.
Taken together, these studies illustrate both the advances
and challenges in OCR and document understanding. OCRbased pipelines remain indispensable for digitizing textual
information and handling language-specific contexts such
as Thai, but they often require sophisticated postprocessing
to overcome recognition errors. OCR-free models such as
DONUT demonstrate promising alternatives for structured
document interpretation and identity verification, yet their
adaptability to domain-specific applications like academic
transcripts remains limited. This gap underscores the need for
tailored OCR frameworks that integrate preprocessing, robust
recognition, and postprocessing to enable reliable verification
in transcript-focused scenarios.
III. M ETHODOLOGY
The proposed methodology for developing an OCR-based
framework for the automated verification of Thai and English academic transcripts at KMITL is organized into five
sequential stages, as illustrated in Figure 1. The workflow
begins with image acquisition, where transcript images are
collected for further processing. This is followed by data
preprocessing, which enhances image quality and prepares the
documents for recognition through techniques such as denoising, binarization, and deskewing. In the information extraction
stage, OCR models are applied to detect and convert textual
content from the transcript images into machine-readable
text. The extracted text then proceeds to the postprocessing
stage, where it is transformed into a structured key–value
JSON format and further refined through error correction
techniques leveraging domain-specific rules, dictionaries, and
statistical models. Finally, the validated JSON output is crossreferenced with the KMITL Registration and Educational
Service Office (KRESO) database, the authoritative source
of student records, thereby enabling automated verification of
transcript content and consistency.
A. Image Acquisition and Data Preprocessing
The process begins with the acquisition of transcript documents, which may be provided as scanned images, PDF
exports, or captured images. In cases where the input is a
PDF file, it is first converted into image format (.png), with
a resolution set at 300 DPI to ensure optimal quality for
subsequent processing.
Once converted, the images are passed through a Transcript
Classification Model to verify whether the input corresponds
to an academic transcript. The dataset used for developing
this model comprised a total of 9,130 images, including 3,510
transcript document from King Mongkut’s Institute of Technology Ladkrabang (KMITL) and 5,620 non-transcript images
collected from various sources. These non-transcript samples

The dataset was divided into training (72%), validation
(18%), and test (10%) sets. A custom deep learning model
based on a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) with a
sequential architecture was employed, consisting of convolutional layers followed by max pooling, flattening, and
fully connected dense layers with ReLU activation. The final
classification layer used a softmax function for binary classification. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer and
categorical cross-entropy as the loss function. The evaluation
results demonstrated a high level of performance, achieving
an accuracy of 99.89% with a loss of 1.41%. If the input
is classified as non-transcript, the system immediately alerts
the user and terminates the process. Conversely, if identified
as a valid transcript, the image is forwarded to the image
enhancement stage.
In this stage, several preprocessing operations are applied
to improve the image quality for OCR. First, watermarks
or colored institutional stamps are suppressed by filtering
high-saturation and high-value pixels and replacing them with
white, thereby enhancing text prominence. Next, brightness
adjustment and illumination correction are performed, followed by noise reduction to further enhance the continuity of
text strokes. Since some fine details may be lost, a character
thickening process is applied to restore clarity and improve
OCR recognition. Deskewing is then carried out by detecting
skew angles via the Hough Line Transform and rotating the
image accordingly. Finally, the document region is cropped
to exclude unnecessary background elements. The results
of each enhancement step are illustrated in Figure 2. After
enhancement, image quality is reassessed to ensure the docu-

ment remains valid as a transcript. If the quality is insufficient,
the user is notified to recheck the input; otherwise, the
document proceeds to the Transcript Language and Degree
Classification Model. This model determines the language
(Thai or English) and the academic level (undergraduate or
graduate). It was trained on 3,510 transcript images evenly
distributed across the four target classes, with additional nontranscript samples included for robustness. The dataset was
divided into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets. A
ResNet-18 architecture with pretrained weights was adopted
as the feature extractor, and transfer learning was applied for
multi-output classification. The model demonstrated strong
generalization ability on the test set, achieving highly satisfactory results for both tasks.
Following classification, the system performs table structure analysis to segment each transcript into four logical regions: header, left body, right body, and footer. Morphological
operations with horizontal and vertical kernels are applied
to detect table lines, followed by region-based cropping.
To address misalignment, the widths of the left and right
body regions are compared, and if a predefined threshold
is exceeded, the middle block is adjusted and re-divided.
Aspect ratios of the cropped regions are also validated to
ensure structural consistency. Validated segments are then
forwarded for subsequent processing, while failures trigger
an error message indicating segmentation issues.
B. Information Extraction
After segmentation, each region enters the Information
Extraction stage, where Optical Character Recognition (OCR)
is applied to convert visual characters into machine-readable
text. To improve reliability, postprocessing techniques such as
dictionary-based spell-checking are employed. In this study,
three widely adopted OCR frameworks were evaluated: EasyOCR, PaddleOCR, and Tesseract. EasyOCR supports more
than 80 languages, including Thai and English, making it
suitable for multilingual tasks with minimal configuration.
PaddleOCR, developed under the PaddlePaddle deep learning platform, provides high-performance recognition across

C. Data Transformation and Error-correction
The outputs generated by OCR models are inherently
heterogeneous and must undergo transformation to ensure
consistency with the database schema. At this stage, errorcorrection mechanisms are also integrated. The extracted text
is reformatted into a structured key–value representation,
enabling direct comparison with reference records stored in
the KRESO database. Specifically, PaddleOCR and EasyOCR
produce structured lists containing bounding box coordinates,
recognized text, and confidence scores, whereas Tesseract
generates plain text without explicit structural annotations.
Consequently, the transformation pipeline is adapted to each
framework.
The process begins with the Header, which serves as a
primary quality indicator. If critical attributes (e.g., student
name or ID) are missing, and if fewer than 70% of required
header fields are successfully extracted, the document is
flagged as low quality and rejected. Otherwise, the Body
and Footer are transformed into the predefined schema. For
PaddleOCR and EasyOCR, line merging is performed based
on y-axis alignment, while for Tesseract segmentation is
guided by predefined keywords (e.g., “Name,” “Student ID,”
“Degree”). Preprocessing operations, including case normalization, whitespace trimming, and removal of extraneous
symbols, are applied uniformly across all frameworks.
Error-correction is integrated throughout the document. In
the Header, fuzzy matching based on Levenshtein distance is
employed to mitigate OCR-induced variations, and similarity
with reference keywords (e.g., institutional name) is accepted
when exceeding 80%. Academic honors are normalized into
numeric codes (e.g., 1 = first-class honors, 2 = second-class
honors), and date formats are standardized by converting
month names and synchronizing Gregorian and Buddhist
calendar years.
In the Body, transcript content extracted from left and right
pages is merged and organized by semester. Core attributes
include academic year, semester, credit units, GPA, course
code, course title, and grade (e.g., A–F, S/U, O, G, P, I,
T(A–B)). Graduate transcripts has additionally specify course
types (e.g., Cr, Nc, Tr), and special cases such as thesis defenses are identified through domain-specific keywords. Errorcorrection in this section emphasizes course code validation:
if a course code matches an entry in the database and the
corresponding course title is sufficiently similar, the system
corrects the course title to align with the reference record.

GPA values and course grades are not altered but reformatted
into standardized patterns (e.g., X.XX), thereby facilitating reliable database-level verification while preserving the integrity
of the original course records. For graduate-level transcripts,
examination-related fields are further validated against the
graduate assessment database.
The Footer includes the date of issue, the name of the
authorized registrar, and the position of the signatory. These
elements are extracted and structured into the schema, after
which error-correction procedures cross-check registrar names
and positions against authoritative records.
Finally, all extracted and corrected information from the
Header, Body, and Footer is consolidated into a JSON structure, producing a standardized key–value representation that
ensures compatibility with automated verification workflows.
IV. E VALUATION
The dataset used in this study comprises synthetically
generated academic transcripts provided by the KMITL Registration and Educational Services Office (KRESO). All documents were anonymized to preserve confidentiality while
maintaining the structural and linguistic characteristics of
authentic records. The corpus covers both undergraduate and
graduate levels, consisting of 211 undergraduate transcripts in
English, 211 undergraduate transcripts in Thai, 140 graduate
transcripts in English, and 140 graduate transcripts in Thai,
for a total of 702 documents. Each transcript follows the
standard structure of an academic record, including header
fields (e.g., student name, program, degree level, graduation
status), body sections (e.g., course lists, credit units, grades),
summary fields (e.g., GPA), and footer elements (e.g., date of
issue, signatory name, and position). Representative examples
of these transcript types are illustrated in Figure 3.
To enhance model robustness and account for potential
variations in real-world scenarios, data augmentation was
applied to the original set of transcripts. Four augmentation
techniques were employed: (i) random rotation within a
range of −10◦ to +10◦ , (ii) noise injection using five noise
types (Gaussian, Salt, Pepper, Salt-and-Pepper, and Speckle)
with varying variances, (iii) brightness adjustment by scaling
image intensity between 20% and 200% of the original,
and (iv) a combined augmentation simultaneously applying
rotation, noise, and brightness adjustment. In total, these
procedures generated 2,808 augmented images. Combined
with the original 702 transcripts, the final dataset comprised
3,510 samples. The details of the augmentation methods and
sample distributions are summarized in Table I.
The original transcript documents, together with their augmented counterparts, were evaluated under four experimental configurations: (i) without image enhancement or error
correction, (ii) with image enhancement only, (iii) with error
correction only, and (iv) with both image enhancement and
error correction. Across all configurations, three OCR models—Tesseract, PaddleOCR, and EasyOCR—were applied to
English transcripts, while Tesseract and EasyOCR were used
for Thai transcripts. System performance was evaluated using

V. C ONCLUSION
This study presented an end-to-end pipeline for transcript
digitization and verification that integrates image enhancement, OCR extraction, and correction for structured database
validation. The framework begins with transcript classification, image enhancement, applies language- and degree-level
classification, and then layout segmentation. The extracted
text is subsequently transformed and corrected to ensure
consistency and accuracy before being verified against the
registrar’s records.
Experimental evaluation on a synthetic dataset of 3,510
transcripts in English and Thai demonstrated the effectiveness
of the proposed approach. Image enhancement substantially
improved OCR robustness—most notably for English with
gains up to +16.51% (Tesseract) and for Thai with gains up
to +11.61% (Tesseract) over raw inputs. Applying correction
further boosted reliability: when applied after enhancement,
improvements reached +14.47% for English (PaddleOCR) and
+21.38% for Thai (Tesseract). Combining both enhancement
and correction yielded the highest overall performance, with
aggregate gains up to +25.75% for English (PaddleOCR) and
+32.99% for Thai (Tesseract) relative to raw inputs. Among
the OCR models, PaddleOCR achieved the best English performance under the combined setting, while Tesseract consistently led on Thai, with EasyOCR showing competitive results
in Thai—particularly under the correction-only condition.
