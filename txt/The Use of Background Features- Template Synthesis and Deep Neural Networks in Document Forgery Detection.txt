Abstract—Document manipulation is a recently arising problem, especially with the rapid spread of fabrication technology.
The tools to alter documents are now publicly available and can
result in high quality forgeries, indistinguishable from genuine
ones. Forged documents may wreak havoc on many processes
dependent on the validity of the document, leading to lasting
consequences such as financial loss. Therefore, the process of
identifying a document that has been altered is essential. A
system that is capable of scrutinizing documents as either forged
or genuine through discriminative features (such as distortions
or character misalignment) can assist industries with heavily
reliance on documents for processes such as identity verification.
Most of the documents involved in such processes have sufficiently
complex backgrounds. We present a computer-vision-based system that detects changes in the background of the aforementioned
documents as a result of manipulations made to its contents
through the use of image subtraction. The system takes an image
as input and then classifies the document as genuine or forged.
Our proposed system produces an accuracy of 95% using CNN
on unaligned images as well as 100% for aligned images.
Index Terms—Document, forgery, detection, genuine, CNN,
background, subtraction, forged, splicing, copy-move

I. I NTRODUCTION
Forgery is the process in which an item in the form
of a document is altered or created to resemble a genuine
counterpart in an attempt to deceive others. Forgeries manifest
from modifications made to existing documents. Determining
whether a document is forged or genuine (see Fig. 1) is crucial
for most verification procedures [1], [2], which is used in most
business aspects and can cause potential data breaches if a
forged document was used as a genuine document. The issue
regarding digital documents unlike the physical documents that
can be inspected closely is that they can be easily modified
with publicly available tooling such as photo editors as well
as scanners and printers [3].
For such types of documents, the text, including the surrounding areas can be valuable indicators of potential forgery.

The techniques used to detect forged documents has been
often partitioned into 2 classes. Active Forgery Detection is the
technique of instilling authenticatable information within an
image or document [6]. This information manifests in the form
of serial numbers, embedded holograms, watermarks, or other
indicators. These forms of authentication are easily verifiable
by the issuing body or creator, but are uncommon and often
reserved for specific forms of documents. An independent
body or party may struggle with verifying these details, as they
are still prone to modification and often require specialized
equipment to be verified.
In contrast, passive forgery detection is the process of
detecting the authenticity of the source image without relying
on embedded data.
Forged images may exhibit evidence of modification despite
resembling its genuine counterpart (For an example, see Fig.
1a and Fig. 1b). Sharma et. al [6] gives the example of
distortion, artifacts and loss of information as symptoms or
clues to a possibly forged document. As depicted in Fig. 1b.
, it can be observed that in most documents with sufficiently
detailed backgrounds, the background of the copied content
does not match the patterns of the surrounding background
in the event of forgery (see Fig. 3). Sharma et. al [6] further

II. R ELATED W ORK
Research involved in such fields investigate common forgery
types that can cause the most danger. Several existing approaches were used to detect such forgeries through the use
of techniques such as optical character recognition (OCR),
statistical models and deep learning models.
A. Classical Approaches
James et al. [4] implemented a random forest based classifier utilizing OCR (using Tesseract [9]) graph features of

vertical displacement relative to its neighbouring characters
on the same line. The proposed system targeted plain digital
documents which lack colors or complex backgrounds. Imperfections in the previously mentioned features indicate areas of
manipulation. The classifier was capable of reaching 98.57%.
Moreover, The authors proposed using pixel-based features
from images along with the OCR graph features in order to
further enhance performance. Osorio et al. [5] proposed an
algorithm that could identify the type of forgery afflicted on the
image using a hybrid model consisting of SIFT and Markov
processing. The algorithm achieved an accuracy of 91%. Ryu
et al. [3] proposed a Support Vector Machine (SVM) based
technique which achieved an overall classification accuracy of
80%. The system aimed to detect printed forged documents
through the inspection of image qualities, along with the
identification of the printer (laser, ink-jet, etc) that was used
to produce a forged document.
B. Deep Learning Based Approaches
Marra et al. [10] proposed a conceptual framework for
the detection of forged images, with special emphasis on
modifications which are not visually obvious. The proposed
system consisted of a CNN which automatically extracts
defining features from an entire image without resorting to
resizing, which is often the cause of loss in detail. The
system was evaluated on a data-set representative of real
forgeries. Additionally, Fahmi & Sari [11] investigated the use
of CNNs of similar architectures for detection of tampered
images. Specifically, the models investigated the effect of an
additional convolutional layer on the pre-existing two layers
on accuracy of the model. The models were evaluated against
various data-sets consisting of images afflicted with copymove or splicing. The most effective model, equipped with an
additional convolutional layer, perform with 83% accuracy on
average. However, the model proposed was suspected to have
overfit. The authors suggested that it could be remedied with
a larger data set with a diverse set of forgeries. Zhao et al. [2]
developed a neural network capable of editing documents with
textual information surrounded by complex backgrounds. The
system is capable of disguising the artifacts produced by the
modification such as mismatching colors or distortion. Tarek
et al. [1] performed a comparative analysis on the accuracy of
detecting forged signatures across multiple CNN models (such
as ResNet50, VGG-16, etc). The results showed that ResNet50 was the highest performing model, producing an accuracy
of 82.3%. Bunk et al. [12] proposed a system using Patch
Classification and LSTM to classify and localize regions in
images that have been tampered. The system has achieved an
accuracy of 94.86%.

B. Deep-Learning Model
The Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) was developed
using the Keras API for TensorFlow [14] and consists of several layers, which are specified in Fig. 6. A data augmentation
layer was used to make random horizontal flips and random
rotations to decrease the rate of over-fitting. Second, a rescaling layer which re-scales the images to the fraction of
1/255 of their size.
The images are then passed into multiple convolutional
layers each followed by a normalization layer. We applied
hyperparameter tuning in order to determine the best dropout
value for the model to minimize over-fitting while maintaining
adequate results. Hyperparameter tuning is the process in
which a search space consisting of all possible values constructed for the set of hyperparameters (such as dropout rate,
kernel size, etc.). The search space is then examined through
random selection or through a bracket style of elimination
to obtain values for each hyperparameter in the model. The
Tuner [15] is responsible for assessing the quality of the model
during the search for more optimal parameter, after a specified
number of epochs, the best model is selected and its values
for hyperparameters are retained. The model was re-trained
after finding optimal hyperparameters on a machine equipped
with 16 GB of RAM, a NVIDIA RTX 3070 TI, and an Intel
Core i9 10900F. The set of forged documents were manually
created with varying levels of detail and quality. A set of
genuine photos were moved into the forged data set before
being manually altered. Each forged document contains one
or more occurrences of pixel manipulation, copy-move and
splicing. Similar to the genuine counterparts, each image was
subtracted from a generated template to expose background
details.

IV. E XPERIMENTS AND RESULT DISCUSSION
Both experiments follow similar setups. First, a template
was generated for a sample of (aligned) genuine documents,
the sample size used for both experiments was 100 images.
After preprocessing the images according to the requirements
of the experiment (see Fig. 4.), the images are assigned to a
training and test set for each type of document after subtraction
from the template, which produces the input to the models.
Each model was evaluated on each type of document model
and their respective accuracy is measured.

A. Experiment 1
The first experiment aims to assess the accuracy of a
selection of models on the post-subtracted images produced
from perfectly aligned images of different types of documents.
1) Setup: The first experiment uses aligned images to test
the aforementioned technique, which was tested on 8 different
types of documents, each containing 132 forged documents
and 162 genuine documents. All models were trained on the
same data set. The models consist of the proposed convolutional neural network (see Fig. 6, and 7 statistical models
listed in Table III. The aforementioned models were selected
to resemble the current state of the art models for binary
classifications as well as selected from the related work.
2) Results: Table II shows the results produced by each
model in each type of document present. Due to the stark
contrast between the noise levels of a genuine and postsubtraction forged image, approaches such as the CNN are
highly effective where as classical algorithms such as SVM
and Naive Bayes perform adequately. Other classical models
have varying performance, which is justified in the remainder
of the section. Regardless of any potential correlations amidst
the features, the Naive Bayes classifier considers each of these
features to contribute independently to the confidence. The
single Decision Tree classifier outperforms a Random Forest
model in terms of average accuracy. This is likely due to
the environment of the experiment, as well as the variance
produced by the differing noise patterns in the forged images,
causing it to underfit. The voting classifier consists of an ensemble of the classical models, wherein the final classification
result is produced from the majority vote of the sub-classifiers.
However the decision is unweighted, therefore many incorrect
classification results produced by sub-classifiers will dominate
the outcome over less votes for the correct result produced for
a particular class.

2) Results: As shown in Table III, overall accuracy produced from experiment 2 has regressed as expected, due
to the noise generated by the alignment errors produced
during registration. Notably, the results demonstrate the weak
generalizability of the Decision Tree. The Random Forest
demonstrated higher accuracy, likely due to the increase in
variation in the data-set overall. This variation has caused the
Naive Bayes to be biased towards producing a classification
result of forged, even for genuine documents due to the alignment noise. Although CNN demonstrated a lower accuracy
than SVM in the second experiment, CNN is likely more
desirable than SVM for this particular use case as it produces
significantly lower false positives (forged documents classified
as genuine) than SVM as shown in the confusion matrices
(generated using [17]) for both classifiers (see Fig. 9.)

V. C ONCLUSION AND F UTURE W ORK
In conclusion, computer vision has the potential to revolutionize the field of document forgery detection by providing
a reasonably reliable and automated way to analyze and
verify the authenticity of a document. Using machine learning,
models can take advantage of various background features
of a document and compare them to known patterns and
characteristics of genuine documents. Two experiments were
conducted; the first experiment processed aligned documents
taken from an artificially sterile environment where no lighting conditions or environmental noise were introduced and
taken into account. The second experiment was conducted
on unaligned documents to measure the efficiency of the
proposed system in real environments where alignment and
environment noise exist. The MIDV-2020 data set was used
for both experiments; forged documents manually created by
mimicking various types of forgeries. Using a set of genuine
documents for each type of document, a generic template
was generated, and then images were subtracted from the
template to construct the genuine and forged data set. The
post-subtraction images were then fed to one CNN model and
six classical machine learning models. The most optimal and
consistent models in both experiments were CNN and SVM,
scoring 100.00% and 96.62%, respectively, in experiment 1
and 95.00% and 97.78% in experiment 2. Despite the fact
that SVM had higher results than CNN, CNN is more reliable
in this particular use case as it produces significantly lower
false positives than SVM which is very sensitive in such field.
Future work includes extending the forged document data set
with more diverse types of forgeries with more environment
noise such as different lighting conditions or the documents
being contorted. The proposed system can be combined with
an OCR-based approach to further improve accuracy.
