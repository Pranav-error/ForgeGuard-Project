Abstract—Auditing ﬁnancial invoices on a massive scale is
a long-standing challenge for the accounting profession due to
the increasing sophistication of contemporary document forgery,
which employs both pixel-level and semantic manipulation. To
overcome this challenge, we propose FORENSIQ, a multi-modal
forgery detection framework. The system dynamically fuses four
distinct streams of information: a six-signal visual forensics
engine, a triple-consensus Optical Character Recognition (OCR)
engine (utilizing Tesseract, EasyOCR, and PaddleOCR), an
arithmetic semantic validator, and a contextual vendor registry.
The architecture achieves robustness through adaptive weighting,
which recalibrates the inﬂuence of each stream based on a computed Image Quality Score (IQS). This enables the framework to
perform effectively even when the input image is of poor quality,
such as a low-resolution photograph captured via a mobile phone.
Evaluated on a comprehensive benchmark of 3,786 ﬁnancial
invoices, our framework successfully detected forgeries with an
85.0% overall accuracy and an AUC of 0.926. Furthermore, the
automated pipeline reduced the average time required to audit
a single invoice from over nine minutes to under 30 seconds.
Index Terms—Invoice fraud detection, document forensics,
multi- modal analysis, optical character recognition (OCR),
invoice auditing, deep learning, ﬁnancial document veriﬁcation

One major blind spot in the document-agnostic nature of the
AI model—i.e., the model examines the invoice but does not

In the Impersonation Attack, the attacker uses a legitimate
invoice from a supplier the company trusts and alters the
supplier’s legitimate GST number with the attacker’s own
using a PDF editing tool. The attacker then sends the invoice
to the company for payment. Since the invoice is legitimate
in every other way and the taxes balance correctly, the visual
and semantic detection systems pass the invoice without any
problems. In our initial tests, we found 11 such invoices
slipped through the image-based and arithmetic mechanisms
undetected.
To prevent the Impersonation Attack, we developed the
Vendor Enrollment Registry. This contextual database stores
legitimate GST numbers, vendor names, and expected billing
ranges. Upon extracting the GST number from an invoice, the
system cross-references it with the registry. If the GST number
does not map to a recognized vendor, the system immediately
assigns a +0.10 anomaly penalty.

II. R ESEARCH G APS AND P ROPOSED B ETTERMENTS
A. Design Choice: Tri-Engine OCR over Single-Engine Extraction
When we conducted our initial tests, single-engine OCR
systems just could not deal with real-world ﬁnancial documents. While running our tests with 500 invoices, Tesseract
alone got around 18% of the amount ﬁelds wrong whenever
fonts got weird or had gradients. EasyOCR was better with
weird fonts but still got 12% wrong—it was really bad with
heavy watermarks. However, the most surprising thing was
that when we used both systems with the same image, their
errors never overlapped. That was our ﬁrst clue that maybe if
we combined them, we could solve our problems. But then
we learned that Silva et al. [6] and his team have recently
discovered that multi-modal models are required if you want
to extract text from all these weird and wonderful invoice formats. So, to complement the errors in Tesseract and EasyOCR,
we have now integrated PaddleOCR into our system as a third
engine and named it the Triple OCR Consensus Engine. The
rule here is quite simple: when all three engines agree on a
value, we set conﬁdence to 1.0. If only two of them agree, we
set it to 0.67. And if none of them agree, we set it to 0.33. This
three-engine solution works much better for messy invoices,
doesn’t need to be trained, and takes only 200 milliseconds
longer than the original solution on a regular CPU.

D. Design Choice: Adaptive Weight Calibration for Scan
Quality
One of the traditional problems in dealing with the practical
processing of operational documents is the variety of media
types presented for processing. For instance, though high-end
ofﬁce scanners produce high-quality scans, invoices scanned
by mobile devices tend to be noisier with many compression
artefacts. Visual forensic techniques like Error Level Analysis
will inevitably detect these noise artefacts, misinterpreting
these as evidence of tampering. For instance, when we used
static weights (wv = 0.68, wt = 0.32) for mobile device
invoices in our test dataset, the false positive rate for legitimate
invoices was as high as 31%. To mitigate this inherent bias
against low-quality photographs, we propose our Adaptive
Weight Calibration System. We calculate our Image Quality
Score (IQS ∈ [0, 1]), which is a weighted average of the
Laplacian variance, indicating sharpness, and the Gaussian
noise residuals, indicating cleanliness. The inference engine
then uses a linear interpolation of these two weights, wv and
wt , based on the calculated IQS. For low-IQS images, we set
(wv ≈ 0.45), reducing the false positive rate of our algorithm
from 31% to 14%.

B. Design Choice: Semantic Gate for Text-Layer Forgeries
One of the biggest weaknesses of purely visual forensic
approaches is that they cannot detect lossless digital modiﬁcations. A skilled fraudster can edit only the text layer of
a digitally created invoice—for example, changing the gross
total—using a PDF editor. Because no pixels are altered, there
is nothing for an image forensic tool to detect. When we
passed our visual stream over a subset of 200 such lossless
PDF-edited forgeries, the score was only 58 %—essentially
random guessing. However, even though the pixel analysis is
valid, the text semantics are often wrong because the attacker
has not recalculated the taxes. This is what motivated the
Arithmetic Semantic Gate. We use OCR to extract the text
and then perform a deterministic arithmetic cross-check using
δ = |G − (N + A)| + |A − N · r/100|, where G, N , A,
and r are the gross total, net amount, tax amount, and tax rate,
respectively. Recent advances in programmatic rule generation
[5] provide a formal basis for this kind of deterministic crossveriﬁcation. Any document where δ exceeds 0.50 is ﬂagged
as Suspicious. This single deterministic rule recovered 91 %
of the lossless-edit forgeries that the visual stream completely
missed.

III. P ROPOSED M ETHODOLOGY
A. Dataset Preparation
It is important to note that a good machine learning model
for forgery detection requires a large corpus of both genuine and fraudulent documents. Since real-world fraudulent
corporate invoices contain conﬁdential information, we customized an approach to obtain robust training data. From the
publicly available SROIE [19] and CORD [16] benchmarks,
we collected 1,814 veriﬁed genuine receipts. To generate the
corresponding fraud class, we utilized three synthetic attacks
simulating real-world methodologies: (A) Copy Move: Digit
regions were duplicated using seamless cloning to overwrite
other amounts. (B) Splicing: Numerals were replaced with
differing font styles and contrasts. (C) Metadata Shift: Images
were re-compressed using various JPEG quality levels to
simulate document laundering. This yielded 1,972 realistic
fraudulent images. This aligns closely with established prior
work on distinguishing near-original replicas and enhancing
machine learning models to detect subtle visual discrepancies
[3], as well as recent tools like AIForge-Doc [1] and ForenXAI
[2]. The ﬁnal 3,786-image dataset was standardized using
bilateral denoising and adaptive thresholding.

F. Adaptive Fusion Classiﬁer
The Adaptive Fusion Classiﬁer takes over right at the end of
the FORENSIQ pipeline. It grabs the results from the visual,
text, and contextual streams, and rolls everything into a single
fraud score, S, somewhere between 0 and 1.
But here’s the thing—it doesn’t just use a ﬁxed formula. It
actually adapts, depending on how good the document looks.
If the document is high quality—like a crisp scan with sharp
details—the visual forensics stream carries the most weight,
with wv set at 0.68. If the quality drops, let’s say it’s a blurry
phone photo, the text and semantic streams step up, and the
text weight, wt , jumps to 0.55.
