Abstract—The abundance of digital and paper document
forgery requires strong automated detection tools against
financial fraud. This research provides a classical machine
learning method for forged receipt detection using multimodal
features from image and text modalities. The approach entailed
designing a feature set to obtain textural and statistical
attributes from receipt images via Local Binary Patterns (LBP)
and Canny edge detection, along with structural features
obtained from the associated text files. Another demanding issue
in this area is the excessive class imbalance between genuine and
forged documents. To overcome this issue, Synthetic Minority
Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) is used to create a
balanced training dataset. The models are assessed using the
macro F1-score, precision, recall, PR AUC and ROC AUC to
address class imbalance. The enhanced detection of the minority
class is achieved using SMOTE, while hyperparameter tuning
leads to the improvements in performance. The final Tuned
Support Vector Machine model achieves a macro F1-score of
0.5429, and it has the highest recall on forged receipts,
demonstrating that it detects more histories of tampered
documents effectively. This research sets a good baseline for
receipt forgery detection and emphasizes that class imbalance
solving is a key towards creating a working system.

II. LITERATURE REVIEW

I. INTRODUCTION
The prevalence of digital imaging and advanced editing
software has caused a tremendous boom in document
falsification, which poses a very serious threat to companies
and financial institutions. The most falsified documents by far
are receipts, which are altered for expense report fraud,
inflation of insurance claims, and tax fraud. The manual
checking of such documents is time-consuming, costly, and
prone to errors. This requires the creation of systems that can
reliably and effectively discriminate between genuine and
counterfeit receipts.
Creating such a system, though, is fraught with several
difficulties. Forgeries are sometimes subtle and multifaceted
and may range from straightforward text manipulations to
sophisticated ``Frankenstein'' documents produced by
combining pieces from various sources. In addition, one of the
main challenges in this space is the natural class imbalance
of real-world data: forged documents simply do not exist
nearly as often as legitimate ones. This data bias can severely
skew machine learning algorithms toward patterns based on

This work presents and assesses a traditional machine
learning workflow for detecting receipt forgery, addressing
these challenges directly. The solution is multimodal,
designing a collection of discriminative features from both
visual properties of the receipt image and structural properties
of the textual content of the receipt. To mitigate the issue of
class imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling
Technique (SMOTE) is used to generate a balanced training
set. A number of machine learning classifiers have been
compared to show that this hybrid approach provides a strong
baseline for the task of forgery detection.
The work is organized as follows: the Methodology
section describes the steps of data preprocessing, feature
engineering, and model training procedures. The Results
section reports the performance of the models. The Discussion
section explains the outcomes and limitations observed in the
research. Finally, the Conclusion section summarizes the
study and outlines how the limitations can be addressed in
future work.

Many researchers have tackled the issue of forgery
detection in various ways, including through pattern-based
analysis, deep learning and the classification of document
features.
Ahsan et al. recognized a major barrier to effective use of
digital forgery detection systems as laboratory-to-practice
differences based on differences in document formats and
image quality [1]. Similarly, Roja et al. described the basic
elements of digital image processing systems and
demonstrates how pattern recognition drives technologies
such as OCR and fingerprint identification—abilities fully
transferable to scanned document forgery detection[2].
Prachi et al. discussed the application of deep learning
approaches, particularly convolutional autoencoders (CAE)
and convolutional neural networks (CNN), to identify
watermark patterns and other repeating structures [3]. The
results demonstrate that deep models are precise but sensitive
to input quality. Mohammed et al. also addressed this issue
by highlighting the limitations of traditional image-based
methods and spectroscopic methods for addressing forgery
[4]. The authors proposed the use of Laser-Induced

Pulkit et al. offered a systematic feature categorization of
document security features—by type, inspection level, and
security level—and examines the implementation in Indian
identity documents [5]. The research emphasizes the
significance of feature-based categorization in the fight
against document fraud, which can inform pattern-based
detection approaches.
Chenfan et al. reviewed current approaches to natural
image tampering and generalizes them to the document field,
proposing the Document Tampering Detector (DTD), a tool
that combines spatial pattern analysis for more sensitive
detection of faint irregularities [6]. Although effective, the
critique points to a lack of solidity in previous methods when
applied across diverse document types.
Jhankar et al. proposed a multi-modal system that
combines visuals, text, and metadata of documents to
improve the accuracy of forgery detection [7]. It notes that
the one-feature systems are more prone to malicious attacks
and format problems and suggests that deep architectures
(such as ResNet-50) are better in complex modern types of
documents.
Joel et al. added seminal contributions to pattern
recognition and deep learning [8]. Joel classifies the learning
approaches—supervised, unsupervised, semi-supervised, and
reinforcement learning—and discusses the nature and
applications of different architectures of deep neural
networks. These concepts are indispensable for
comprehending how deep learning models can be utilized
successfully in scanned document analysis and forgery
detection.
Pushpalata et al. and Xin et al. (2023) also added to this
literature by concentrating on domain-specific datasets and
enhancing training resilience, though those results are not
investigated here in detail [9][10].
Kilari et al. used CNN in the research to detect copy-move
forgery in digitally modified identity documents[11]. By
applying the CNN on the MICC-F2000 datasets, training
accuracy of 98.33%, 100% precision, and an F-score of 98%
was obtained. When applied to a self-created dataset,
IDCCMFD-1000, the CNN reported a training accuracy of
92% and a testing accuracy of 93.02%. These results
demonstrate that the CNN model was easily transferable
across different datasets.
In another study, Aboudramane et al. presented Docpatch, an unsupervised method to detect forgeries in
documents without the use of labeled training or testing data
[12]. Doc-patch uses FAISS K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) to
compare embeddings to a memory bank of verified authentic
documents, and documents are identified as forged if the
anomaly scores exceed a defined threshold. Doc-patch
outperforms GANomaly, a popular unsupervised baseline, by
a significant margin.

Naglaa et al. presented the model IMD-CNN, using
unique spectral signatures of the inks to facilitate
identification and detection of document forgeries [13]. This
approach not only detects forged documents, but also
determines the number of inks within the document, and
detects forgeries by locating the regions that have been
manipulated within a document. The results show that the
model correctly identified forgeries with 99.91% accuracy
for blue ink and 97.51% accuracy for black ink, surpassing
previous work by 0.18% (blue ink) and 0.36% (black ink).
Teerath et al. implemented another approach to overcome
issues with the limited number of datasets available to
researchers and the problems associated with annotation by
developing a four-step algorithm for creating synthetic forged
documents[14]. The authors employed a fill-in-the-blank
approach, where data is filled into documents with various
characteristics, characters are randomly altered on different
occasions by misalignment of the document or the addition
of background noise, augmentation was applied to augment
the collection of the synthetic forged document, and the
authors would retain the bounding box information for the
annotations into a JSON file.
A dataset was created by Beatriz et al. the same one
utilized in this study, by conducting workshops where 19
compatriots of varying ability in editing created and
annotated forgeries, and experimentation of the dataset
reported that traditional methods such as Bag-of-Words with
Logistic Regression (F1: 17.78%) and SVM on raw pixels
(F1: 13.33%) performed poorly [15]. In comparison,
ChatGPT in a zero-shot condition was able to achieve greater
recall but very low precision (F1: 28.39%). The best
performance came from a CNN that was trained to detect
JPEG compression artifacts, which attained around 78-79%
precision.
III. METHODOLOGY
With the purpose of developing a strong classification
model to differentiate forged receipts from authentic ones,
traditional machine learning techniques were employed, with
feature engineering from both image and text sources. The
main steps consist of data preprocessing, multimodal feature
extraction, handling major class imbalance, model training,
and thorough evaluation of the models.
A. Dataset Structure
The dataset used in this study is the Find it again! dataset,
obtained from Kaggle[16].
The dataset contains receipt images in .png format and
corresponding extracted text in .txt format, obtained using
Optical Character Recognition (OCR). In addition, the
dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets,
which are specified in the files train.txt, val.txt, and test.txt,
respectively. This division ensures that the models do not
carry forward any bias from the training data.
B. Feature Engineering
For the feature set, both visual and structural properties of
the receipts were considered and extracted.

1) Image Feature Extraction:
Visual evidence often serves as a primary forgery
indicator. To extract such information, features were
computed from the grayscale version of each receipt image
resized to a standard 256 × 256 pixel size.
•

Texture Analysis: Local Binary Patterns (LBP) was
used to examine the textural characteristics of the
receipts. LBP defines image pixels by thresholding
neighboring pixels and encoding results as binary
values. A 10-bin histogram of the LBP result was
calculated to produce a compact texture feature
vector.

•

Statistical Features: Basic image statistics,
including the mean and standard deviation of pixel
intensities, were calculated to capture information
about contrast and brightness.

•

Edge Density: The Canny edge detector was
applied to each image to identify edges. Edge
density, defined as the number of edge pixels
divided by the total number of pixels, was used as a
feature to detect irregular patterns or artificial lines
introduced through tampering.

2) Text Feature Extraction
The layout and wording of the text on a receipt may also
provide clues of tampering. The following text-based features
were extracted:
• Token Count: Total number of words.
• Digit Count: Total number of numeric digits.
• Average Token Length: Mean length of tokens.
• Keyword Presence: Binary indicators of whether
essential keywords such as “total” and “date” are
present, which are fundamental to a valid receipt.
These image and text features were concatenated to form
a single feature vector per sample.
C. Managing Class Imbalance via SMOTE
Initial exploration revealed a significant class imbalance
between genuine and forged receipts, with the authentic class
dominating. Training directly on such imbalanced data risks
producing models biased toward the majority class. To
address this, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling
Technique (SMOTE) was applied.
SMOTE creates synthetic samples of the minority class
by randomly selecting an instance, finding k nearest
neighbors, and generating a synthetic sample along the line
segment joining the instance and one neighbor. This
augmentation was applied only to the training set, ensuring
no information leakage into validation or test sets.
D. Training Models and Tuning Hyperparameters
Two approaches were taken for training:
1. Training on original data with class weight
adjustments.
2. Training on the SMOTE-balanced dataset.

The four algorithms evaluated were: Logistic Regression
(a basic linear classifier), Random Forest (an ensemble of
decision trees), Support Vector Machine (SVM), which finds
a separating hyperplane, and XGBoost, a gradient-boosting
framework.
For raw-data models, class weighting parameters such as
class_weight='balanced' (Logistic Regression, Random
Forest, SVM) and scale_pos_weight (XGBoost) were used to
mitigate class imbalance. For SMOTE-balanced models, a
StandardScaler was applied to normalize training data, which
was later used to transform validation and test sets.
After preliminary evaluation, the models showing the best
performance (Random Forest, XGBoost, and SVM) from the
SMOTE pipeline were re-evaluated for hyperparameter
optimization. Randomized Search Cross-Validation
(RandomizedSearchCV) was employed, optimizing for the
macro F1-score to achieve balanced performance across both
classes.

IV. RESULTS
In this section, the empirical results of the experiments are
presented. The performance of four machine learning models
under three different conditions have been compared: (1)
training on original imbalanced data, (2) training on SMOTEbalanced data, and (3) training the best-performing models on
SMOTE-balanced data with hyperparameter optimization.
The macro F1-score is used as the main metric for model
comparison, as the dataset is highly imbalanced.
The first evaluation considered the performance of models
trained on the original imbalanced dataset. Class weighting
techniques were employed to handle the imbalance.
The results in Table 1 show that despite relatively high
accuracy, the models were unable to detect the minority class
(``Forged''). For example, the Random Forest model achieved
an accuracy of 83.49% but completely failed to identify forged
receipts (recall = 0.00). Logistic Regression and XGBoost also
performed poorly in terms of recall and F1-score for the
forged class, highlighting the limitations of class weighting
alone.
To improve detection of the minority class, models were
retrained on data balanced using SMOTE.
As shown in Table 1, nearly all models trained with
SMOTE exhibited improved recall and F1-scores for the
forged class compared to those trained without SMOTE. For
example, the Random Forest model's F1-score improved from
0.4550 to 0.5111, and it was able to correctly classify some
forged documents (recall = 0.1429). Similarly, the SVM
model improved from an F1-score of 0.4003 to 0.5035. These
results demonstrate the effectiveness of SMOTE in enhancing
classifier balance.
The three best-performing models from the SMOTE
experiments—Random Forest, XGBoost, and SVM—were
further tuned using Randomized Search Cross-Validation.
Among these, the tuned SVM showed the most notable
improvements, with its F1-score increasing from 0.5035 to
0.5429 and PR AUC rising from 0.1835 to 0.2464. Based on
these results, the tuned SVM trained on SMOTE-balanced
data was selected as the best-performing model. It achieved
the highest macro F1-score (0.5429), the highest PR AUC
(0.2464), and the highest recall for the “Forged'” class
(0.3143), indicating the most balanced and effective
performance across all models.
The quantitative results are further supported by the
Precision-Recall (PR) curves in Figure 2. Models trained with
SMOTE (right plot) show superior curves compared to those
trained without SMOTE (left plot). The curve for the tuned
SVM (green line, AUC = 0.2464) is the most prominent,
visually confirming its superiority for this classification task.

The experiments yielded several important insights,
particularly regarding the necessity of addressing class
imbalance.
The baseline models performed poorly despite the use of
class weighting. For instance, the Random Forest classifier
achieved an overall accuracy of 83.49%, but its recall for the
“Forged” class was zero. Thus, it provided no value for the
primary task of detecting forgeries. This result exemplifies
one of the pitfalls of classification on imbalanced datasets:
accuracy can be misleading when one class dominates the
distribution.
On the positive side, applying SMOTE improved the
performance of all models, particularly in terms of F1-score

and PR AUC, which are more appropriate measures for
imbalanced problems. By generating synthetic minority
samples, SMOTE enabled the models to better capture the
distinctive characteristics of forged receipts instead of
defaulting to the ``Authentic'' class. This demonstrates that
balanced training data is essential for forgery detection, and
that class weighting alone is insufficient.
The multimodal feature set proved effective, highlighting
the value of incorporating both visual and textual features.
Visual features such as LBP and edge density capture
artifacts of tampering and irregular textures, while textual
features such as token counts and keyword presence reveal
logical inconsistencies introduced during electronic edits.
The combination of these features created a richer

Finally, the tuned SVM emerged as the best-performing

Although ensemble models like Random Forest and

B. Limitations of the Study
While the approach was successful, several limitations
remain. The methodology depends on manually engineered
features. This approach may be less powerful than end-to-end
deep learning models (e.g., CNNs), which can learn complex
representations directly from raw data.
The accuracy of text-based features depends on the
quality of the OCR process. Errors or noise in OCR output
can significantly reduce detection performance in real-world
settings.
The feature set, while effective, remains relatively simple.
Advanced techniques such as semantic NLP analysis, EXIF
metadata inspection, or Error Level Analysis (ELA) were not
explored.
The model was trained and tested on a single dataset. Its
performance may not generalize to receipts from other
domains, languages, currencies, or forgery techniques. Realworld forgeries may differ significantly from those in the
dataset.
VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This study highlights the possibility of using a standard
machine learning pipeline for detecting forged receipts using
multimodal features from both the images and text. An
important finding was that addressing class imbalance is
crucial, where class weighting was used. On the other hand,
using SMOTE helped improve minority class representation.
The Support Vector Machine trained with hyperparameters
achieved the best performance of all models with a macro F1score of 0.5429, which is an informative benchmark for
future work.
For future work, there are several directions to further
expand this line of work: build end-to-end deep learning
architectures that integrate convolutional neural networks
(CNNs) for extracting features from images along with
transformer-based models for text, use OCR integrated with
forgery detection to allow for noise present at the character
level, develop a dataset composed of various formats and
types of forgery, and study forensic features like Error Level
Analysis and font consistency. These avenues could generate
higher quality representations and more robust generalizable
models for detection of document forgery.
