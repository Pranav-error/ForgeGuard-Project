I. INTRODUCTION
The swift digitalization of financial solutions has
revolutionized the customer onboarding process to
allow the user to provide identity documents fully
online. Although this change enhances convenience
and speed of operation, it also demands new security
threats. The forgeries of documents based on AI are
becoming more and more authentic, and traditional
OCR systems, as well as the simplified CNN-based
models, are less useful to detect the manipulation.
This increases the risks of identity theft, fraudulent

regulations by the financial institutions. Such
difficulties underscore the necessity of more
sophisticated AI-based verification methods that can
be used to identify subtle pixel, texture, and
structural anomalies that can be used to tell the
authenticity of documents and their fake
counterparts. The given study is the answer to these
issues as it will present a Python-based document

II. LITERATURE REVIEW
Digital Identity Verification Systems Timeline
The developmental history of digital identity
verification systems has been developed by the high
rate of online financial services and the need to
onboard processes without facing issues of insecurity
and inconvenience. At first, it was verified by manual
methods of the review mechanism, during which
users submitted identification documents and
checked them by human operators. Though this
method was accurate to some degree, it was not
scalable and had a high susceptibility to the human
factor, delays, and inefficiency in operations. Despite
the growth of digital banking, fintech platforms, and
the necessity to ensure the reliability and automation
of verification emerged, and this resulted in the usage
of Optical Character Recognition (OCR) and simple
image processing tools [2]. The methods retrieved
the textual data and matched it to databases which
became the first step to automation in verifying
identities. Nonetheless, document manipulations or
high-quality forgeries would remain unnoticed by
OCR-based systems and thus they would not be
effective in curbing sophisticated fraud methods.
The second wave of development was the
incorporation of the Artificial Intelligence (AI) and
Machine Learning (ML), which allowed systems to
examine patterns, textures, and anomalies within the
digital documents more efficiently. By detecting the
visual inconsistencies that the traditional algorithms
were not able to identify, CNNs revolutionized the
process of document verification. Artificial
intelligence systems also increased their verification
speed and flexibility and minimized manual
interference [1]. Regardless of these developments, it
was still weak in addressing adversarial attacks and
forgeries that are of deepfakes nature and replicates
the authentic features with high accuracy. The latest
and radical change in digital identity verification is
the transition to Generative AI (GenAI). The GenAIbased systems achieve semantics understanding at
the complexity of visuals and pixel-level detection of
synthetics or manipulated material by utilizing the

Data Mining weaknesses on CNN and OCR
Methods
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Optical
Character Recognition (OCR) systems yield
important results when it comes to authentication of
documents; however, they have exactly outlined
weaknesses as far as complex and developing
document forgeries are concerned. Text extraction is
the primary objective of OCR systems, and they are
mostly based on the recognition of characters instead
of their authenticity. They may effectively scan and
computerize data that can be found on identity
documents, but cannot detect more covert visual and
visual manipulation, including forged seals, font
modifications, or holograms. Consequently, the
verification of documents using OCR usually
accommodates counterfeit document which looks
real especially when complex image editing software
are applied so as to maintain structural consistency
[7]. Furthermore, accuracy in OCR reduces with
poor scan quality, blurred pictures or with
multilingual texts, which is frequent in international
digital onboarding practices. The worst improvement
came with CNN-based methods which analyzed both
the pixel and feature level thus allowing automated
pattern and irregularity recognition.

Forgery Detection Generative AI
Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) is now a
new change maker in the subject of document
forgery detection, overcoming the drawback of
traditional OCR and CNN-based systems. In contrast
to conventional models, which utilise features
extraction and classifications only model, GenAI
applies advanced models, including diffusion
models, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs),
and transformers, to learn, generate, and analyze
document structures on a pixel basis [5]. This
generation power enables the system to model the
realistic and advanced distribution of documents and
it is very successful in detecting the tiny changes on
the textures, the background, and the security
patterns. Teaching GenAI to understand how the
appearance of genuine documents ought to look like
helps identify even the smallest defects that may be
overlooked by the human eye of regular algorithms.
Practically, GenAI models are trained on identity
document data on a large scale i.e., passports,
licenses, financial statements etc., which is how they
learn high-dimensional visual representations. After
training, such models have the potential of recreating
or forecasting the anticipated image outlines of
authentic papers and contrasting the documented
outputs with them.

Nevertheless, CNNs have some fundamental
weaknesses in their reliance on large, heavily
labelled datasets and inability to effectively
generalize to unseen forms of forgery. They tend to
ignore contextual relations or semantic discrepancies
between document modules because they pay
attention to spatial hierarchy. Moreover, CNNs are
susceptible to adversarial examples, i.e., malicious,
imperceptible manipulations of images to find them
offensive to models and interpret them in the wrong
category. Such a weakness follows a decrease in their
strength in the face of actual forgery attempts where
the fraudsters deliberately structure the deceptive
inputs to avoid detection mechanisms. The other
issue is the impossibility of CNNs and OCRs to
adhere to generative forgeries made with the help of
the latest AI tools [4]. Manipulations of the
documents of the Deepfake type and manipulations
of the textures are not always evident, because these
models do not generate the knowledge of the data

Blockchain Authentication of Documents
Document verification aided by blockchain
technology enables a decentralized and non-erasable
method of document verification that introduces a
new trust and transparency to the system of digital
identification and verification to lessen the risk of
identity theft. Unlike centralized databases that are
prone to unauthorized access and manipulations,
blockchain can archive verification records in an
unchangeable record in a distributed ledger that
spans across various nodes. Every transaction which
is a verification or creation of a document is signed
cryptographically and time-stamped and a change is
noticed instantaneously. This contributes to the
efficiency of blockchain specifically in ensuring the
provenance of documents, avoiding duplication, and
authenticity validation in real-time digital settings. In
conjunction with the AI-based forgery detection
systems, blockchain will increase the overall stability
of the verification pipeline [10]. Generation AI
models have the ability to scan document
characteristics and identify possible forgery and
afterwards it is written on an encrypted cryptographic
registry network that could be used in future. The
integration will create a continuous auditable audit
trail, which will offer a higher level of regulatory
compliance, especially where Know Your Customer
(KYC) functionality and anti-money laundering
(AML) measures are available.
A document is validated and the hash of its own or a
digital fingerprint is stored on blockchain thereby,
with the aid of such authentication, the institutions
will be able to verify whether a document is authentic
in real-time without the necessity to access an
original file. Also, it becomes possible to automated
verification working flows with the help of the smart
contract functionality of blockchain. These contracts
may be coded to execute particular actions i.e.
document approval or rejection upon satisfaction of
some preexisting authenticity conditions. This saves
time that would be spent on doing things manually,
it saves time spent in processing and one can do away
with human error. Financially, blockchain-based
authentication enables the customers to have more
control of their data since they can give or take access
to authenticated credentials safely [9]. The
combination of the blockchain and Generative AI
forms a powerful hybrid carcass of document
authentication schemes. As GenAI provides a
verification of the accuracy of detection of forgeries
at both visual and contextual scale, blockchain
provides integrity and traceability at the level of the
system as a whole. The combination of both creates
an overall defence system that does not only deter

document manipulation, but also creates a longerterm confidence in online financial systems.
III. METHODOLOGY
Overview
The methodology is to create a smart system that can
identify forgery in the documents, in the process of
online onboarding, through the use of Generative
Artificial Intelligence (GenAI), computer vision, and
digital forensics. This paper is concerned with
building an effective Python-based model, which
simulates the behaviour of a GenAI-driven model of
forgery detection. It involves the data preprocessing,
deep learning model training and visualisation to
detect pixel-level and structural anomalies between
authentic documents and forged ones. The data is
composed of three major document types (bank
statements, passports, and driver licenses) put in
labelled folders to recreate the conditions of actual eKYC. The individual data sets are transformed and
augmented to have a stable learning process and to
improve the generalisation of the model under
different document types.
Every image is preprocessed, with transformations
to input data including resizing and converting
tensors standardising input data to be used in
training. This guarantees that the deep learning
model gets normalised and uniform images that are
utilized in accurate feature extraction. In order to
build the learning base, the approach uses a
convolutional neural network, namely ResNet18 that
was selected due to the combination of simplicity and
efficiency in its performance [25]. The model will be
set to run offline eliminating reliance on cloud based
APIs and be trained with an established number of
epochs to guarantee optimal convergence [12].
Evaluation mechanisms, optimiser, and loss method
in the methodology are the key elements. The
classification accuracy is calculated using the crossentropy loss, and the Adams optimiser is used to
optimise the network to ensure a quick convergence.
The model is trained to reduce the loss progressively
by comparing the predicted results to actual
document labels during the training stage, and
progressively enhance its sensitivity to detect a minor
inconsistency.
The model is stored to be used later, and the
parameters are learned so that they can be used to
process new data without re-training. The methods of
visualisation are used to show both the performance
in learning and sample predictions. Training loss
curve gives graphical analysis of optimisation of
model in each epoch, and random dataset represents
data diversity and integrity of preprocessing [11]. All
in all, the presented methodology presents a
replicable framework along with a structure that
proves how GenAI-inspired models can be applied to
document forgery detection to establish a basis of
future
application
with
blockchain-based
authentication frameworks.

Generative Model Implementation
Besides the ResNet18 classifier, a generative model
was also applied to increase the detection of formerly
unknown forgeries. Convolutional autoencoder was
chosen because it is stable, has a lightweight
structure and is applicable in reconstruction-based
anomaly detection. Only real documents of passport,
driver license, and bank statement datasets were used
to train the autoencoder that was able to extract the
underlying visual format, texture, and structural
layout of authentic samples. The decoder is used to
reconstruct each image input in the inference phase
and a reconstruction error score is computed using
pixel-wise mean squared error. Veritable documents
give low values to the error, whereas forged
documents give considerably larger values to the
reconstruction errors because of the inconsistencies
that cannot be reproduced by the model. This
generative process is used in addition to the classifier
to detect less evident manipulations that have never
been detected before.

PyTorch was used to develop a convolutional
autoencoder that would aid in the detection of fragile
and hitherto unknown document forgeries. It is a
model that includes an encoder, which reduces the
size of input images into a small latent representation
and a decoder, which re-generates the original image
using this latent representation. The autoencoder was
only trained on real samples of passports, driver
licenses, and bank statements and it utilized the
similar structural and textual patterns in the real
sample, making it learn only. In the process of
inference, every input image is given to the
autoencoder and a reconstruction error score is
obtained by the comparison between the output and
the input. Authentic images create low errors and
fake documents create higher errors because of
incongruency that model is incapable of creating.
This form of generation approach supplements the
classifier as it allows the detection of anomalies.

Training and Visualization Process Model
The reliability and interpretability of forgery
detection system is ensured by taking a systematic
approach to model training and visualisation. The
model is trained to learn ways of distinguishing
between authentic and counterfeit documents,
through the recognition of minute pixels-level
anomalies. The training pipeline created in Python
with the help of PyTorch fits the purpose of
improving the methods of digital identity verification
based on Generative AI-assisted deep learning. The
algorithm starts with loading the processed images of
the three datasets, which include the passports,
drivers license, and bank statements, into a
DataLoader, and it will arrange the images into
batches to ensure that it is efficient in the usage of
computation time. The batches go through a tailored
ResNet18 model that is programmed to do binary
classification. In every forward pass, the model
makes predictions on whether an image of a
document is authentic or not.
Adam, the optimiser, then gradually updates the
network weights to minimise this loss and thus
achieve more accurate models with each successive
epoch. This is repeated by a set number of epochs
until the model has learned the characteristics of
documents including edges, textures and printed
patterns. The loss per epoch is summed up and saved
to be visualised during training. Observation of the
loss assists in assessing convergence behaviour as
well as avoiding problems such as overfitting and
underfitting. As highlighted in the paper, model
performance involves visual representation which is
a key factor in the understanding of the performance
of models. Matplotlib is then used to create a loss
curve illustrating the reduction in the error rate of the
model as the number of epoch runs increases, after
training [17]. This curve graphically illustrates the
process of learning and assists in proving that the
model is developing consistently. The paper contains
visualisation of random samples of the documents to
further justify the database and preprocessing steps.
Such images are depicted in a grid manner with
authentic and counterfeit images with the labels of
each. This kind of visualisation will provide the data
diversity guaranteeing that the transformations
(resizing and normalisation) have been implemented
correctly. The optimised model is stored to be
deployed and tested in the future [18]. Using
quantitative measurements alongside visual analysis
the paper makes sure that not only a model training
process leads to accuracy, but also transparency of
the visualisation process. This extra-layered method
makes the system more trustworthy to the real-world
application of digital onboarding and KYC
verification.
