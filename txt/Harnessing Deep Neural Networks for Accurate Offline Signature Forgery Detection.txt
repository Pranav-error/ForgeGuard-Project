Abstract—S ignature is considered to be one of the broadly
practiced and important biometric features for the verification
of an individual. It is used in various documents like pan card,
passport, bank cheques, etc which can lead to numerous frauds
like identity theft, money laundering and so on. To help solve
or reduce this issue, an attempt is being made to develop an
offline signature forgery detection system using an artificial
neural architecture known as the Siamese neural network which
consists of two identical Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)
which are trained to learn feature representations of genuine
and forged signatures. The two individual Siamese Models
implemented in our project provide an accuracy of 98.27%
and 98.35% respectively. Additionally, on occasions one of the
models performs better while the other gets confused. To get
optimum results from the former, a logical system was developed
for handling the corner cases which resulted in an accuracy of
99.75%

of writing one’s in itials or name to p rovide authorizat ion
in cheques, debit cards, property papers and so on. Forgery
detection is the process of authenticating if a given signature
matches with a person’s original sign. In other words,
signature verification is performed to avoid forgery while
cashing cheques, transferring property or simply accepting
couriers.
There are two types of signature verification systems:
Online and Offline verification. Online verification makes

It is difficult fo r an individual to provide the exact same
signature over mult iple attempts and this discrepancy can be
taken advantage of by a skilled forger [3]. The problem to be
tackled here is to take into consideration the subtle changes in
an individual’s signature over mu ltiple attempts and the fake
signature a forger makes and finally to train a model wh ich
knows the difference between a genuine and a fake signature.
To avoid this kind of forgery, a Siamese neural network [4]
was constructed which consists of two identical convolutional
neural networks (CNN) [5] [6] which are trained with the
same weights to identify if a signature is real or fake. The
dataset in use consists of mu ltip le attempts made by a single
person and also a bunch of forged signatures of the same
person.

A. Dataset Description

Navid S M A, Priya S H, Khandakar NH, Haque AB,
Ferdous Z [9] constructed a model called VGG-19 which is
constructed on pre-trained CNN. They made use of ICDAR
as their dataset which yielded an accuracy of 100%, when
CEDAR was used accuracy resulted to 88%, and 94.44%
for Kaggle based dataset, to verify if a g iven signature is
genuine or not. They faced co mputing resource limitat ions
and were unable to work with larger datasets which they plan
to overcome in the near future.

The ”Signature Verification Dataset” [14] which contains
the signatures of Dutch Users for checking forgery is used
to train the model was sourced from Kaggle [15], a popular
data science community that provides a wide range of
tools and resources for data analysis and machine learning.
This dataset consists of signatures of Dutch users that were
collected specifically for the purpose of detecting forgery. The
signatures were obtained fro m the ICDA R 2011 Signature
Dataset [16].

Hafemann L G, Sabourin R, Oliveira L S [10] approached the
problem using feature learning perspective. Their hypothesis
included learning features fro m the data instead of hand
crafted ones which have no resemblance to the signature
generation process. They intend to exp lore the importance of
a good method for defining user-specific thresholds as their
methods achieved low Equal Error rates. Also the actual False
Rejection and False Acceptance rates are very imbalanced,
and not stable across multiple users and datasets.

The offline dataset comprises PNG images that were scanned
at 400 dpi and in RGB color, and was orig inally used in a
signature verification competit ion organized by the German
Research Center for Art ificial Intelligence in collaboration
with M Liwicki in 2011. The dataset is organized into two
main directories: Train and Test. Each directory includes two
subdirectories for each user, namely ”Genuine” (with the user
number) and ”Forgery” (with the user nu mber + ” forg”).
There are a total of 128 subdirectories (64 subdirectories
containing genuine signature samples and 64 subdirectories
containing forged signature samp les of 64 users) and 42
subdirectories(21 subdirectories containing genuine signature
samples and 21 subdirectories containing forged signature
samples of 21 users) present in the Train and Test directories,
respectively. Please note that each subdirectory may contain a
variable nu mber of signature samples. Additionally, the Train
and Test directories do not overlap in terms of users, meaning
that the users in the Train directory are distinct fro m those
in the Test directory. No additional preprocessing or cleaning
steps were performed on the dataset.

Xiao W, Ding Y [3] p roposed a method using twostage Siamese neural network model for accurate offline
handwritten signature verificat ion which adopts a two -stage
Siamese neural network to verify original and enhanced
handwritten signatures simultaneously, and also utilizes the
Focal Loss to deal with the extreme imbalance between
positive and negative offline signatures.
R. K. Bharathi B. H. Shekar [11] p roposed a model
which uses eigen-signature construction They have worked
on converting scanned signature images to a shape form and
eigen-signature construction is proposed for extracting the
feature vector fro m a shape formed signature. The proposed
system is conducted on a newly created Kannada offline
signature database.
Boubacar Diallo, Thierry Urruty, Pascal Bourdon, Christine
Fernandez-Malo igne [12] identified the problem of images
present on online sharing platforms which have high
probabilit ies of undergoing transformation like resizing,
filtering which may impose many constraints on forgery
detection algorithms. They proposed a camera identification
model based on CNN.
Ane Bro mley, Isabelle Guyon, Yann LeCun, Eduard Sickinger
and Roopak Shah [13] proposed an algorithm for verify ing
signatures based on a Siamese neural network which consists
of identical sub-networks joined at their outputs. They hope
to have a field trial in the future wh ich can be run in order
to test this technology in the real world.

Additionally, the dataset includes two separate CSV
files for the training and testing data, containing 23,205 and
5,747 rows respectively. These CSV files were developed
by randomly selecting a combination of two images fro m
either the genuine or forgery directories, belonging to either
the same or d ifferent user. The CSV files are arranged
systematically and consist of three fields: the path to the
two images, and a label indicating whether the images are
classified as genuine or forged.
B. Pre-Processing
In order to prepare input images for signature forgery
detection using the Siamese network, it is necessary to
perform pre -processing steps. These steps typically involve
resizing, grayscaling, and binarization of the scanned images
of signatures. The purpose of this pre-processing is to
reduce the complexity of the input images and imp rove the
computational efficiency of the Siamese network.
Image Resizing: To ensure consistency and imp rove the
performance of the Siamese network, wh ich includes a CNN,
all input images are resized to a standard resolution of 128

Image GrayScaling: Image grayscaling is the process of
converting a color image into a grayscale image, where each
pixel in the grayscale image represents the luminance or
brightness of the corresponding pixel in the o rig inal color
image. Th is is achieved by taking the weighted average of
the red, green, and blue color channels of an RGB image.
Grayscaling is performed to simplify the image representation
and reduce the computational cost required for processing.
Additionally, it also contributes towards enhancing certain
features of an image, such as edges and textures, making
them easier to detect.
Image Binarization: Image b inarizat ion is a process that
involves converting a grayscale or color image into a binary
image by apply ing a threshold value. Pixels with values
below the threshold are set to 0, while p ixels with values
above the threshold are set to 1. Although there are several
methods to determine the appropriate threshold value for
different applications, the OTSU thresholding method [17] is
often used to determine it automatically. Th is pre-processing
step is performed to standardize the pixel values, which can
improve the performance of CNN models.

The Siamese network consists of two identical convolutional
neural networks (CNNs) that share the same weights and
are trained to learn feature representations of genuine and
forged signatures. The selection of a CNN-based approach
for signature forgery detection is justified by its ability to
learn discriminative features fro m the raw image data, and
its proven success in a wide range of image recognition tasks.
The output of the two CNNs (the pecuilar number of neurons
present in each CNN layer is shown in Fig. 5 and Fig. 6
and the reason behind the selection of neurons is described
latter part of this section) is then concatenated and passed
through two dense layers that contain 64 neurons and 1 neuron
respectively. These dense layers serve the purpose of introducing non-linearity to the feature representation learned by the
CNNs. The first dense layer contains 64 neurons and uses the
Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function. ReLU is a
commonly used activation function in neural networks because
it is co mputationally efficient and allows the model to learn
complex non-linear relationships between features. The second
dense layer contains a single neuron and uses the sigmoid
activation function. This layer is responsible for co mputing
the dissimilarity score between the two signature images. If
the dissimilarity score is below a predefined threshold, the
signature is classified as genuine, otherwise, it is classified as
forged. A threshold value of 0.5 is chosen because it represents
a natural decision boundary between the two classes. When the
output of the Siamese network is greater than or equal to 0.5,
it means that the dissimilarity between the two signatures is
high, and the network classifies that the signature was forged.

V . CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE
Our experimental results demonstrate that our Siamese
network-based signature forgery detection system described
in this study achieves a high level of accuracy (99.75%), indicating the effect iveness of our proposed approach in detecting
signature forgeries. The novelty of our proposed model lies in
the use of a Siamese network with two separate sub-networks.

We utilized two distinct convolutional layer architectures for
each sub-network to handle the issue of the relative positions
of the two images in input pairs, which can affect the accuracy
of the model. The final predict ion is obtained by combining
the outputs of the two sub-networks using a custom function
that takes into account the relative positions of the two
images. The neural network approach to automated signature
forgery detection is a significant advantage, as it eliminates
the need for manual inspection by experts, which can be timeconsuming and costly. However, a d isadvantage of this model
is that the features it uses to produce the expected output
are not known. Nevertheless, the model provides objective
and consistent evaluation, unlike human experts who may
be influenced by biases and contextual factors. Additionally,
signature forgery detection models are useful for quickly and
accurately verifying a large number o f signatures, making
them ideal fo r high-volu me processing in banks, financial
institutions, or legal proceedings.
The proposed system can be used to provide API services
for signature authentication and verificat ion. To authenticate
a signature using an API, the user would need to provide
two signature images intended for verificat ion, along with any
relevant in formation, such as the name of the signer or the
date it was signed, and the API would then process the image
and indicate whether the signature is genuine or forged.
To further enhance the reliab ility of the model, a hard ware
setup such as a tablet could be utilized to detect and verify
signatures based on factors like pressure points and writ ing
style. When a person signs their name on a tablet, the device
captures various parameters like signature shape, movement,
pressure points, speed, and acceleration of the stylus [22]. By
incorporating these parameters and training the model with
signature images, the credibility of the model’s predict ions
can be significantly improved.
Our current signature classification model is designed to
determine the authenticity of Dutch signatures and has b een
found to work well with English signatures as well, given the
similarity between Dutch and English writing styles. However,
for languages written fro m right to left, such as Arabic and
Hebrew, the direction of strokes could be a crucial factor in
determining the authenticity of the signature. Conversely, for
languages like Chinese, Japanese, and Korean, the structure
of characters and strokes could be more relevant in detecting
forgery. To analyze these differences accurately, more data
would be required, and the model could be further developed
to cater to various languages.
