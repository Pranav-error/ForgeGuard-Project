Abstract—Signatures are vital for identity veriﬁcation in banking, legal agreements, and document authentication, making their
authenticity crucial due to rising fraud. Traditional methods
address either signature recognition or forgery detection but
independently, with no uniﬁed framework. In this work, we
propose a hybrid model for signature veriﬁcation that uses deep
learning models, CNN and InceptionV3. We initially collect the
publicly available GDPS-100 ofﬂine signature dataset CustomCollected Signature Dataset. After preprocessing, the dataset is
split into training, testing, and validation sets. We train the CNN
model to identify signatures from the identity of the individual,
and at the same time we train the InceptionV3 model to identify
genuine and forged signatures. Person identiﬁcation is done
using CNN because it has the capability to learn distinctive
handwriting characteristics, and forgery identiﬁcation is done
using InceptionV3 because it has the capability to learn features
at multiple scales and is robust at detecting subtle differences. We
then merge these two models into a single pipeline, which when
provided with an input signature image, identiﬁes the person
and whether the signature is genuine or forged. The experiments
show that this strategy effectively solves the person identiﬁcation
and forgery detection tasks, and it provides a uniﬁed and efﬁcient
solution for real world signature veriﬁcation.

II. L ITERATURE R EVIEW

Signatures have been a safe form of identiﬁcation for
decades of individuals in various industries, such as banking,
court forms, and government processes. Their handwritten
nature gives them a personal touch to veriﬁcation. Yet, as technology advanced, so has the technique of forging signatures,
rendering manual checks less reliable. There are methods
such as expert forgeries, where a signature is mimicked after
rigorous practice, and even AI forgeries that almost appear to
be real. These limitations introduce the demand for a stronger
and more automated system of signature veriﬁcation to deter
identity theft and document forgery.
Traditional signature veriﬁcation methods are generally biased towards signer identiﬁcation or forgery detection [1], and
they treat them as separate tasks. Though attempts at both
are addressed in some works, these are tried with parallel
models with minimal integration and lack the leverage to gain
from shared information between the two. The difference is
not suitable for real-world scenarios where a signature can

Support Vector Machine (SVM) and K-Means algorithms that

lack the ability to handle all the variations in different signatures. The performance can also decrease when it is applied to

health, which are not taken into consideration, so more ﬂexible
systems need to be maintained.
Hashim et al. [5] performed a comparative analysis of
machine learning algorithms for handwritten signature veriﬁcation with the models SVM, CNN, K-NN, and DTW.
They compared over 20 studies in terms of dataset, feature
extraction, and classiﬁcation methods. Despite tremendous
advancements, challenges like high intra-class and inter-class
variability of signatures, lack of adequate genuine signature
samples for training, and poor detection of skilled forgeries
were presented, which indicated the necessity for more reliable
systems capable of accommodating varied signature styles and
enhanced forgery detection.
Wang et al. [6] focus on advances in ofﬂine handwritten
signature recognition, both traditional feature extraction and
modern deep learning-based methods. They document the
use of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to feature

like Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) and Radon
transform. There are a number of challenges in spite of these
advances, according to the paper, like variability in signature
styles and lack of dynamic characteristics in ofﬂine signatures,
which make them more difﬁcult to verify compared to online
signatures. Challenges like sparse feature representation in
ofﬂine signatures, lack of large, diverse training datasets,
and multilinguality across signature samples are discussed,
pointing towards the requirement of more robust and wellintegrated systems in real-world applications.

IV. M ODEL A RCHITECTURE
The system proposed integrates signature veriﬁcation and
forgery detection into a single framework of deep learning. It
employs the CNN and InceptionV3 models to offer precise,
dual-output veriﬁcation from signature images.

Fig. 1. Model Architecture

Figure 1 provides the architecture of this Signature Veriﬁcation System, covering the end-to-end process from data
acquisition to end output. With the integration of the strengths
of CNN and InceptionV3 models, and training on dual
datasets, the system is highly accurate and reliable. The
end output provides both person identiﬁcation and signature
genuineness, thus forming a complete and scalable solution for
practical implementation of signature veriﬁcation in real-world
applications such as banking, legal documents, and identity
veriﬁcation services.
The system design is an integrated and modular deep
learning system for raw signature image processing, extraction
of meaningful features, and production of correct identity
veriﬁcation and forgery detection results. Signature data is
collected from two data sources: the GPDS-100 publically
available ofﬂine signature dataset and a Custom-Collected
Signature Dataset of signatures of 19 individuals. Raw image
data is subjected to a systematic Data Preprocessing process

A. Data Description
The raw signature images used as the source in this study
are derived from two diverse datasets, processed individually
to offer diverse training and testing. The GPDS-100 dataset
contains 100 individuals with each having 54 signatures (24
real and 30 forged) with a total of 5,400 images. The CustomCollected dataset contains 19 individuals with each having
approximately 140 signatures (80 real and 60–80 forged) with
a total of 3520 images. The datasets are taken individually for
model training and testing to offer improved generalization
and accuracy in signature veriﬁcation.

C. CNN for Signature Recognition
The CNN architecture is a 14-layered network of convolutional, pooling, ﬂatten, dropout, and dense layers that is
speciﬁcally designed for grayscale signature recognition with
an input dimension of 150×150 pixels. Beginning with four
convolutional blocks with an increasingly greater number of
ﬁlters (4, 8, 16, 32), the network continues with max-pooling
layers that reduce spatial dimensions and preserve salient
features. This structured deep feature extraction pipeline facilitates ﬁne stroke pattern and texture variation extraction crucial
for signature discrimination.
As shown in Figure 3, the architecture then transitions into
a dense structure comprising a ﬂattening layer and three fully
connected layers with units 256, 128, and 100, respectively. A
dropout layer is introduced before the ﬁnal classiﬁcation layer
to minimize overﬁtting and improve generalization across
unseen data. The ﬁnal dense layer outputs 100 units, each
corresponding to an identity class. This architecture supports
both discriminative feature learning and robust classiﬁcation
performance.

Fig. 5. Multi-Task Parallel Hybrid Architecture Workﬂow

As illustrated in the Figure 5, the architecture begins with
a single RGB input signature image. This image is passed
through two preprocessing pipelines—one that converts the
image to grayscale and resizes it to 150x150 pixels for the
CNN model, and another that resizes the RGB image to
299x299 pixels for the InceptionV3 model. These parallel
branches independently process the input through their respective models to generate feature outputs. The CNN branch
outputs the predicted person (identity), while the InceptionV3
branch determines the signature’s authenticity (genuine or
forged). Both outputs are then fed into a ﬁnal stage where they
are merged to provide a comprehensive result that includes
both the person’s identity and the authentication status.
This hybrid strategy of combining both paths signiﬁcantly
enhances classiﬁcation efﬁciency and ensures even higher generalizability across diverse handwriting styles and complexities
of signatures. Low- and high-level feature learning separation
enables the model to be highly generalizable and attuned to
ﬁner variation in signature patterns. Additionally, architectural
modularity enables smooth scalability and optimization, in
which it is well-positioned to be a versatile tool in realworld signature veriﬁcation systems, where fraud detection
and identity veriﬁcation are of paramount concern. Blending
recognition with forgery detection into a single framework, the
architecture exhibits efﬁciency and wisdom through the use of
multitask learning.

D. InceptionV3 for Forgery Detection
The forgery detection model builds on the powerful InceptionV3 architecture, which serves as the core feature extractor.
It begins with an input layer accepting preprocessed images
of size 299×299 pixels. These inputs are then passed through
InceptionV3, a deep convolutional neural network pretrained
on ImageNet, capable of extracting rich hierarchical features
from the signature images.
The output from InceptionV3 is ﬂattened into a onedimensional vector, which enables its use in fully connected
layers. As illustrated in Figure 5, this representation is processed by a dense layer followed by dropout to prevent
overﬁtting. Another dense layer further reﬁnes the features,
again followed by dropout. A ﬁnal dense output layer applies
a softmax activation function for classiﬁcation, yielding the
forgery detection result.

The hybrid deep learning model combining CNN and InceptionV3 has demonstrated strong performance in signature
recognition and forgery detection. Using CNN for signer identiﬁcation and InceptionV3 for forgery detection, the system
accurately classiﬁes signatures. Results from both the custom
dataset and GDPS-100 validate the model’s efﬁciency, with
higher accuracy observed in the custom dataset (140 samples
per signer), highlighting the importance of a larger sample
size for improved performance. This indicates that the model
is better able to capture intra-class variations, which enhances
its ability to generalize across different signature samples.
Looking forward, the model can be improved by incorporating a wider variety of signature styles, multiple scripts,
and languages to boost generalizability. Future advancements
may include exploring cutting-edge techniques like attention
mechanisms or transformer-based models. Additionally, deploying the system on web or mobile platforms with secure
cloud integration could bring practical applications in ﬁelds
like banking, legal services, and authentication.
