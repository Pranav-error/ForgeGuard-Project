Abstract—Handwritten signature authentication is crucial in
sectors like banking, security, and education, where identity
verification plays a significant role. Signatures are a unique
proof of authorization, making their verification essential to
prevent forgery and fraud. This study presents a deep learning
based approach for handwritten signature verification using
transfer learning with the VGG16 model. The model extracts
key distinguishing features from hand written signatures and
applies a classification method that compares new signatures
against stored references using specific threshold values. A wellcurated data set undergoes pre-processing to enhance feature
extraction before being utilized for training and evaluation.
To improve accuracy and robustness, the model incorporates
data augmentation techniques that help mitigate variations in
handwriting styles and distortions. Adaptive thresholding ensures
flexibility in signature matching, reducing false rejections and
improving overall reliability. The experimental results highlight
the effectiveness of the proposed model, demonstrating a high
level of reliability and precision in distinguishing between genuine
and forged signatures. This approach provides a robust solution
for safe and accurate signature verification in real-world applications, offering significant improvements over traditional manual
verification methods.
Index Terms—Transfer Learning, VGG16, Deep Learning,
Signature Authentication, Forgery Detection, Feature Extraction,
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Document Security, Identity Verification.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Handwritten signatures have long been recognized as a fundamental means of authentication in various domains, including banking, legal documentation, and educational institutions
[1]. They serve as a unique representation of an individual’s
identity and authorization. However, with the advancement of
forgery techniques, traditional signature verification methods
relying on manual inspection and expert judgment have proven
to be subjective, time-consuming, and prone to inaccuracies
[2]. The need for an automated, efficient, and reliable verification system has therefore become crucial.

Signature authentication is broadly categorized into two
types: static (offline) and dynamic (online) verification [3].
Offline authentication, which is the focus of this study, involves verifying a signature after it has been completed by
comparing it against previously stored reference signatures.
Despite various algorithms being developed for signature verification, many existing approaches have struggled to achieve
reliable performance, often yielding results below the 80%
mark [4]. This challenge arises due to the complexities of
handwritten signatures, variations in individual writing styles,
and the limitations of traditional classification techniques [5].
By allowing models to recognize intricate patterns and
distinguish features from signature images, the development
of artificial intelligence and deep learning has completely
changed the process of verifying signatures [6]. Convolutional
Neural Networks (CNNs) are ideally suited for examining the
complex features of signatures because of their impressive
performance in computer vision tasks [7]. Specifically, transfer learning has become popular since it makes it possible
to optimize pre-trained deep learning models for signature
verification without the need for large datasets [8].
This system uses the VGG16 model, a popular pre-trained
CNN architecture, to propose a deep learning-based method
for handwritten signature verification. The model successfully
distinguishes between authentic and fake signatures by utilizing VGG16’s deep feature extraction capabilities [9]. Adaptive
thresholding techniques are used to compare new signatures
with reference samples for authentication, and signature images are pre-processed to improve feature extraction [10]. The
effectiveness of the suggested method is assessed through
thorough testing, which shows that it is highly reliable and
accurate in differentiating genuine signatures from fakes [11].
The findings demonstrate how deep learning can improve
security and effectiveness in signature-based authentication
systems [12].

II. R ELATED W ORK
The field of signature verification has a rich and evolving
history, transitioning from traditional manual inspection methods to highly advanced automated techniques. In the early
stages, signature verification relied on visual and statistical
analysis performed by experts who examined specific features
such as stroke direction, pen pressure, and the time taken to
complete the signature. While effective to some extent, these
manual methods were inherently subjective, time-consuming,
and prone to errors, particularly when dealing with skilled
forgeries or variations in an individual’s signing style. Furthermore, manual verification required extensive expertise, making
it unsuitable for large-scale authentication tasks in domains
such as banking, legal documentation, and education [1] [2].
To address the limitations of manual verification, early
automated techniques were introduced, relying on statistical
and rule-based approaches to analyze signature features. These
methods extracted key attributes such as stroke curvature,
pen pressure distribution, and geometric characteristics of the
signature. However, such techniques faced challenges due
to the natural variability of handwritten signatures, as even
genuine signatures from the same individual could exhibit
differences based on factors like writing speed and pen grip
[3] [4]. Additionally, many statistical methods required specialized hardware for data acquisition, particularly in dynamic
signature verification, where real-time pressure and motion
data were needed [5].
As computational power increased, machine learning techniques became widely adopted for signature verification. Algorithms such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Hidden
Markov Models (HMMs), and Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)
improved verification accuracy compared to traditional statistical techniques. However, they relied heavily on feature
engineering, where domain experts manually selected and
extracted relevant features from signature images [6]. The
performance of these models depended on the quality of
extracted features, making them less adaptable to variations
in signature styles. Moreover, these machine learning models
often required large labeled datasets for training, making them
resource-intensive [7].
With the emergence of deep learning, signature verification has undergone a significant transformation. Convolutional

Neural Networks (CNNs) have demonstrated remarkable success in recognizing complex visual patterns, making them
highly effective for analyzing handwritten signatures. Unlike traditional machine learning approaches that depend on
handcrafted features, CNNs automatically learn hierarchical
representations from raw signature images. Several studies
have explored different CNN architectures, including Siamese
networks and triplet loss-based learning, to develop more robust verification systems [8]. Siamese networks, for example,
have been widely used to learn similarity metrics between
signature pairs, allowing the model to compare signatures
directly rather than classifying them into predefined categories
[9].
One of the most impactful advancements in deep learning
for signature verification is the adoption of transfer learning.
Transfer learning enables models pre-trained on large-scale
datasets, such as ImageNet, to be fine-tuned for signature
verification with relatively smaller datasets, reducing the need
for extensive labeled data while improving model performance
[10]. Among various deep CNN architectures, VGG16 has
emerged as a strong candidate for feature extraction due to its
deep hierarchical layers that capture fine-grained visual patterns. Originally designed for large-scale image classification,
VGG16 has been successfully repurposed for various computer
vision applications, including handwritten signature analysis
[11].
Building upon these advancements, our work explores the
use of VGG16 for feature extraction in combination with
an adaptive thresholding classification method. By leveraging VGG16’s deep feature extraction capabilities, the model
efficiently differentiates between genuine and forged signatures without requiring extensive manual feature engineering
[12]. The extracted features are further processed through an
adaptive thresholding technique, which dynamically adjusts
the decision boundary based on signature similarity, ensuring
robust and reliable verification [13]. This approach enhances
accuracy and improves the model’s ability to generalize across
different handwriting styles, making it a viable solution for
real-world applications in banking, education, and security
domains [14]. Through rigorous experimentation and evaluation, we aim to demonstrate the effectiveness of this deep
learning-based signature verification system, highlighting its
potential to enhance security and efficiency in signature-based
authentication processes [15] [16].

The methodology for handwritten signature verification
follows a structured approach to ensure accurate and reliable authentication. It begins with dataset preparation, where
genuine and forged signatures are collected, preprocessed,
and augmented to enhance model training. Next, feature
extraction is performed using the VGG16 model, leveraging
transfer learning to obtain high-level signature representations.
These features are then compared using similarity metrics,
with adaptive thresholding improving classification accuracy.
Finally, the model is trained using a classifier, optimized

A. Dataset Preparation
Data Collection: A carefully curated dataset is crucial for
training and evaluating the model. This dataset should
contain a sufficient number of genuine signatures from
each individual, along with a representative set of forgeries. The forgeries should encompass both skilled and
random forgeries to challenge the model’s ability to
discriminate between genuine and fraudulent signatures.
For this project, we have used a dataset obtained from
Kaggle, which provides a diverse collection of handwritten signatures. This dataset serves as the primary
source for training and testing the model, ensuring a
comprehensive evaluation of its performance.
• Data Pre-processing: The signature images undergo several pre-processing steps to enhance feature extraction
and improve the model’s performance:
Grayscale Conversion: Converting the images to
grayscale reduces the dimensionality of the input data
and simplifies the analysis. Image Resizing: Resizing
the images to a consistent size ensures that the model
receives inputs of the same dimensions. A size of
224x224 pixels is typically used for compatibility with
VGG16. Image Normalization: Normalizing the pixel
values to a specific range (e.g., [0, 1]) helps to improve
the model’s training stability and convergence. Data
Augmentation: Techniques like rotation, scaling, and
translation can be employed to artificially increase
the size and diversity of the training data, reducing
overfitting and improving generalization.

Transfer Learning: We utilize the VGG16 model, pretrained on the ImageNet dataset, as a feature extractor.

the learned weights and features from the ImageNet
dataset.
• Feature Extraction Layer: The output of a specific layer
in the VGG16 model (e.g., the last convolutional layer
or a fully connected layer) is used as the feature vector
for each signature image. This feature vector represents a
high-level representation of the signature’s characteristics.

C. Classification and Verification
Similarity Metric: A similarity metric, such as cosine
similarity, Euclidean distance, or other suitable measure,
is used to compare the feature vector of a test signature
with the feature vectors of the stored reference signatures
for the claimed identity.
• Thresholding: A threshold value is defined to determine
whether the test signature is considered genuine or a
forgery. If the similarity score between the test signature
and the reference signatures exceeds the threshold, the
signature is classified as genuine; otherwise, it is classified as a forgery.
• Adaptive Thresholding: Instead of using a fixed threshold,
an adaptive threshold is calculated for each individual
based on the distribution of similarity scores between
their genuine signatures. This allows the system to account for individual variations in signature style and
improve accuracy. One approach is to calculate the mean
and standard deviation of the similarity scores for genuine
signatures of each individual and use a threshold based
on these statistics.
•

D. Model Training and Evaluation

fully connected classifier followed by a sigmoid activation function. The training process fine-tunes the weights
in the classifier while keeping the VGG16 weights frozen.
• Parameter Optimization: The classifier is trained with the
preprocessed dataset and the Adam optimizer is used to
minimize the binary cross-entropy loss.

IV. R ESULTS
The implementation of the handwritten signature verification system was carried out in two phases: one using
only a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and the other
incorporating a pre-trained VGG16 model combined with
a CNN. The results from both approaches were evaluated
based on accuracy, reliability, and efficiency in distinguishing
genuine and forged signatures. The CNN-only model achieved
moderate accuracy, ranging between 75-78%, but struggled
with complex variations in signature styles, requiring extensive
labeled data for effective learning. In contrast, the pre-trained
VGG16 model significantly improved feature extraction, leading to superior classification accuracy. The dataset was divided
into 80% training and 20% testing using the train test split
method, and the VGG16-based model achieved an impressive
98.6% accuracy, demonstrating high reliability in detecting
forged signatures.

This study presents a deep learning-based approach for
handwritten signature verification using transfer learning with
the VGG16 model. Signature authentication is essential in
security-sensitive areas such as banking and legal documentation, where preventing forgery is crucial. Traditional methods often struggle with variations in signatures and skilled
forgeries, making deep learning a viable solution. The proposed model leverages the VGG16 architecture to extract key
distinguishing features from handwritten signatures. A wellbalanced dataset of genuine and forged signatures is used for
training, with pre-processing techniques like grayscale conversion, image resizing, and normalization applied to enhance
feature extraction. Data augmentation methods such as rotation
and scaling further improve generalization. The verification
process involves comparing a new signature against stored references using similarity measures and threshold-based classification. Experimental results show that the model achieves high
accuracy in distinguishing genuine signatures from forgeries.
Two experiments were conducted: one using a CNN alone and
another integrating a pre-trained VGG16 model. The latter
outperformed the former, confirming the benefits of transfer
learning. The findings highlight the model’s reliability and
efficiency, making it suitable for real-world applications like
financial transactions and identity verification. This approach
strengthens security by reducing fraud risks. Future work could
explore additional deep learning models and larger datasets to
enhance accuracy and robustness further.
