Abstract—Identity cards, academic certificates, invoices, bank cheques and signed legal pages are increasingly submitted as scanned or photographed images, and inexpensive editing and generative tools have made them easy to forge. Manual verification is slow and inconsistent, while a single forged credential or contract can cause serious financial and legal harm. This paper presents a comprehensive analysis of AI-based approaches for detecting and verifying document forgery according to forgery type, evidence source, detection technique and verification mechanism. The reviewed approaches include Siamese and metric-learning signature verification, splicing and copy-move localisation, noise-residual and error-level analysis, OCR-based semantic and arithmetic validation, multimodal fusion, anomaly detection and blockchain anchoring of credentials. Representative results on CEDAR, BHSig260, CASIA, the Forged Scanned Document (FSD) dataset, MIDV-2020 and invoice corpora are compared with emphasis on accuracy, F1 score, IoU and AUC. The analysis also discusses challenges related to clean-scan tampering, small and imbalanced datasets, explainability and the integrity of verification records.

Index Terms—document forgery detection, signature verification, image tampering localisation, deep learning, OCR, multimodal fusion, explainable AI, blockchain.

1. Introduction

Digital onboarding, online admissions, e-KYC and remote legal processes now depend on documents that are uploaded as images rather than inspected in person. Identity documents, degree certificates, transcripts, invoices and contracts are verified by staff who rarely have forensic training and who must process large volumes quickly. At the same time, image editors, inpainting tools and generative models allow a forger to replace a name, alter an amount or paste a signature with little visible trace. Maintaining document integrity is therefore essential for any institution that accepts digital submissions.

Document forgery takes several forms. A handwritten signature may be traced or skilfully imitated; part of an image may be spliced from another source or copied within the same page; printed text may be replaced or inpainted; and the content of a field may be altered so that the document remains visually plausible but semantically wrong. Prior work has shown that each of these forgeries leaves different evidence, so no single detector covers them all [7], [18], [19].

Document images also differ from the natural photographs on which most forensic methods were developed. They have plain backgrounds, regular printed text, stamps and handwriting, and they are often re-scanned or re-photographed, which removes compression artefacts. Consequently, AI-based techniques that combine visual, textual and structural evidence have gained importance for document verification.

The remainder of this study is structured as follows. Section 2 presents related works grouped by theme. Section 3 describes the taxonomy of document forgery, the benchmark datasets and the main AI-based detection approaches. Section 4 presents the comparative analysis of representative methods. Section 5 presents the problem statement and the challenges identified from the reviewed literature. Section 6 concludes the study with future research directions.

1.1. Background and Motivation

A forged document may look entirely normal to a human reviewer while carrying a single altered field, such as a date of birth, a grade or a total amount. Because the forged region is small and the rest of the page is genuine, whole-image classification often fails, and localisation of the edited region becomes as important as the final decision. Recent surveys of splicing and tampering detection therefore emphasise pixel-level localisation and the fusion of learned and hand-crafted forensic cues [7], [14].

1.2. Types of Document Forgery

Forgeries may target the signature, the image content, the text, or the document as a whole. Signature forgeries are random, simple or skilled imitations of a genuine signature. Image-level forgeries include splicing, copy-move, inpainting and recompression. Text-level forgeries replace or alter printed characters, while semantic forgeries change values so that fields become inconsistent with one another, for example a total that no longer equals the sum of its parts [6], [19].

1.3. Detection Challenge

The main detection challenge is identifying small, deliberate edits on documents that are captured under varied conditions, without rejecting genuine documents that are merely noisy, skewed or re-scanned. Detection approaches therefore examine handwriting features, noise residuals, compression traces, keypoint correspondences, OCR text, field formats and arithmetic consistency [5], [18], [20].

2. Related Work

Recent research has investigated signature verification, image tampering localisation, document-level forgery detection and credential verification. The studies reviewed in this paper are grouped according to these four themes.

2.1. Offline Signature Verification

Emberi et al. built two Siamese networks of twin CNNs with tuned hyper-parameters that compare a questioned signature with a genuine one, reaching 98.27% and 98.35% accuracy, and a rule layer for disagreements raised this to 99.75% [1]. Tehsin et al. fused hand-crafted and one-dimensional CNN features in a bagged ensemble and outperformed earlier systems on BHSig260, CEDAR and SigComp2011, but their writer-dependent model must be trained per signer [2]. CALSigNet combined a CNN, an attention unit and an LSTM for stroke sequences and reported 99.60% test accuracy with a model of only 1.55 MB, although its five-fold mean of 95.44% suggests over-fitting [3]. Irene and Surendran trained a CNN with an ArcFace angular-margin loss and used Grad-CAM to show that decisions rest on meaningful strokes, achieving an AUC of 0.9194 on unseen writers [4]. Mistry et al. are among the few who locate signatures on real multilingual Indian documents, detecting them with YOLOv5 at 92.71% mAP and removing 82.39% of background noise before matching [5]. Nandanwar et al. detected altered handwriting in noisy and blurred images using conformable moments and a deep ensemble with steganalysis kernels [6].

2.2. Image Tampering Detection and Localisation

A survey of splicing detection from 2003 to 2022 concluded that CNN-based methods outperform traditional cues and that hybrids of both are promising [7]. Ahmed localised spliced regions with a U-Net using an EfficientNet encoder [8]. Prabu et al. exploited double-compression traces with a lightweight network and reached 95.87% validation accuracy, but such traces are absent in lossless scans [9]. Bisht et al. showed that A-KAZE keypoints detect copy-move forgery with 98.98% accuracy on NB-CASIA at the lowest computational cost [10]. Aryan et al. proposed a patch-based Vision Transformer with only 69,793 parameters, reaching F1 scores of 0.9587 on CASIA v1 and 0.9914 on DEFACTO [11]. These methods are evaluated on natural photographs rather than on documents.

2.3. Document-Level Forgery Detection

Saire and Tabbone detected counterfeit banknotes and identity documents from a single low-resolution patch with a residual network [12]. Hamido et al. subtracted a questioned identity document from a synthesised template and classified the difference with a CNN, reaching 100% on aligned and 95% on unaligned MIDV-2020 images [13]. Jaiswal and Singh released the FSD dataset of 6,656 forged scanned documents with pixel masks; the best baseline, an attention U-Net, reached an IoU of only 0.55 [14]. Situ et al. designed a two-branch perceptual hash that combines OCR text encoded by BERT with visual features and detects tiny text edits missed by image hashes [15]. Neerakan et al. showed that a classical receipt-forgery pipeline reaches a macro F1 of only 0.5429 under class imbalance [16]. Sampathkumar and Kotha paired a ResNet18 classifier with an autoencoder that flags unseen forgeries during e-KYC, obtaining 94.8% accuracy and an AUC of 0.92 [17].

The strongest recent systems fuse several kinds of evidence. Koul et al. combined a MobileNet visual stream with attention, an edge stream and an OCR embedding stream, improving robustness to print-scan recapture and font tampering [18]. FORENSIQ verifies invoices with a triple-OCR consensus, an arithmetic gate and ELA heatmaps; the full system reached 85.0% accuracy and an AUC of 0.926 against 74.2% for visual-only detection [19]. Vijesh et al. combined ELA, U-Net localisation and metadata checks into a 0–100 authenticity score with an F1 of 92.5% [20], and ForensicDocAI joined ELA-CNN with Grad-CAM, OCR rules, ResNet-50 signature verification and tamper-evident logging [21].

2.4. Credential Verification and Blockchain

Wattanacheep et al. verified Thai and English transcripts with OCR and post-correction, reaching about 82% accuracy [22]. SRM-Net localised inpainting in academic certificates with fixed Spatial Rich Model filters and a ResNet-18 RGB stream, achieving an F1 of 0.91 where ELA failed on clean scans [23]. Alam et al. issued certificates with hashes and QR codes anchored on a blockchain and verified them with OCR and fuzzy logic at 97.2% accuracy [24], while Mishra et al. added a CNN-BiLSTM pre-validation step so that forged certificates are rejected before being anchored [25].

3.3. Noise-Residual Tamper Localisation

Error-level analysis and double-compression methods rely on JPEG artefacts that disappear when a document is scanned losslessly or saved at high quality [9], [20]. SRM-Net addresses this with two streams: fixed high-pass SRM filters expose the noise residuals left by inpainting, while an RGB stream supplies semantic context; the two are fused in an encoder-decoder that outputs a pixel mask [23]. 3.4. Semantic and Arithmetic Validation

Some forgeries leave no visual trace, for example a digitally generated invoice with a wrong total. OCR-based validation extracts names, dates, identity numbers and amounts, checks them against format rules, and verifies arithmetic such as gross equals net plus tax [19], [21]. Triple-OCR consensus reduces recognition errors, which matter because OCR accuracy on real transcripts is only about 82% [22].

3.5. Integrity and Blockchain Anchoring

Perceptual hashes and blockchain records protect documents after they are issued [15], [24]. However, a ledger proves only that a registered document has not changed; it cannot judge a document from another issuer, and an AI check is still needed before anchoring [25].

3.6. Evaluation Metrics

The reviewed studies report different metrics, which makes direct comparison difficult. Signature verification is usually reported as accuracy, area under the ROC curve (AUC) and equal error rate (EER), the point where false acceptance equals false rejection [1], [4]. Tamper localisation is measured at the pixel level with precision, recall, F1 score and intersection over union (IoU) between the predicted and true masks [14], [23]. Document-level systems report accuracy, F1 and AUC [19], [20]. Accuracy alone can be misleading when forged samples are rare, as the low macro F1 of the receipt study shows [16]; precision, recall, F1 and AUC on held-out data give a more reliable picture.

4. Comparative Analysis

The existing studies use different evidence to identify forged documents. One group classifies or verifies a single modality, such as a signature crop or a whole image, whereas another group localises tampering at the pixel level or fuses several checks. Methods differ in the evidence they use, whether they localise the forgery, and whether they explain the decision.

4.3. Key Findings

Four broad findings emerge. First, signature models achieve very high accuracy on clean benchmark crops, but very few locate the signature on a real page [1], [3], [5]. Second, tamper localisation on documents remains difficult, as shown by the low IoU on FSD, and methods that depend on compression artefacts fail on clean scans [9], [14], [23]. Third, fusing visual, textual and structural evidence clearly outperforms any single stream; in FORENSIQ the full system gained more than ten points over visual-only detection [18], [19]. Fourth, many reported accuracies come from small or imbalanced datasets, and realistic evaluation lowers performance sharply [16].

4.4. Research Gaps

Comparing the reviewed studies reveals several gaps that remain open:

  • Clean-crop assumption: signature studies rarely detect and clean the signature on a real page before verification [1]–[4].
  • Natural-image bias: tamper localisers are mostly validated on photographs, not on scanned documents [8], [10], [11].
  • Artefact dependence: ELA and double-compression cues fail on clean or lossless scans [9], [20].
  • Opaque output: many systems return only a genuine-or-forged label that a reviewer cannot defend [12], [25].
  • Unseen forgeries: supervised models learn only the forgery types seen in training, which motivates an additional anomaly detector [17].

5. Problem Statement

The reviewed literature identifies four focused problems:

  • Localisation on real documents: Most tamper detectors are tested on natural photographs, and on the only public document benchmark pixel-level localisation remains weak, especially when no compression artefacts are present.
  • Signatures in context: Signature verification is usually evaluated on clean crops, while practical systems must find, clean and verify a signature on a full page using only a few references per signer.
  • Single-modality decisions: Image-only or text-only systems miss forgeries that leave no visual or no textual trace, so evidence must be fused into one consistent decision.
  • Explainable and auditable output: A practical framework must justify its decision to a non-expert reviewer, be evaluated with precision, recall, F1 and AUC on realistic data, and keep a tamper-evident record of each verification.

6. Conclusion

Document forgery poses a significant threat to institutions that accept digital submissions, because small and deliberate edits can make a forged credential, invoice or contract appear genuine. The reviewed studies cover CEDAR, BHSig260, CASIA, FSD, MIDV-2020 and invoice and certificate corpora, and examine Siamese signature verification, splicing and copy-move detection, noise-residual localisation, OCR-based validation, multimodal fusion and blockchain anchoring. Reported results show that individual techniques are strong on clean benchmarks but rarely combined on full-page documents or explained to the person who must act on them. Overall, effective verification requires on-page signature verification, noise-residual tamper localisation, semantic and arithmetic checks, and an explainable, auditable fused score. Future work can focus on larger real-world document datasets, anomaly detection for unseen forgery types, support for Indian scripts, and blockchain anchoring of verified records.
